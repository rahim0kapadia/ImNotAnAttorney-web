### Post Content

His mother was the one who figured it out.

He was 22. First arrest. Drug charges. His public defender had 300+ cases and about 7 minutes a week to think about his. Every time he called, he got a paralegal. Every time the paralegal called back, the update was the same: "Nothing new."

His mom couldn't sleep. She started researching. Not law school research, just a regular person with an internet connection and a son whose freedom was on the line.

She learned about something called "chain of custody." She learned that every time evidence changes hands, from the officer to the evidence room to the lab, it has to be documented. She learned that breaks in that chain can be challenged.

She asked her son to ask his attorney: "Is the chain of custody intact for the evidence in my case?"

His attorney didn't know. Hadn't checked. Hadn't thought to check, with 300 other cases demanding attention.

But once the question was asked, the attorney looked. And the chain of custody had a gap. The evidence sat unlogged for 72 hours between the arrest and the lab submission. No documentation of where it was, who had it, or what happened to it during that time.

The attorney filed a motion to challenge the evidence integrity. The prosecution's case didn't collapse entirely, but it weakened. Significantly. Enough to change the plea negotiation from a felony with prison time to a misdemeanor with probation.

His mom's research didn't replace the attorney. It prompted the attorney to do the work that should have been done from the beginning.

That's the thing nobody tells family members: you are not helpless in this. You can research. You can learn what questions matter. You can bring those questions to the attorney and say: "What about this?"

You are not the defendant's lawyer. But you might be the reason the lawyer does their job.

(This story is a composite based on common experiences. It is not about a specific individual. This is information, not legal advice.)

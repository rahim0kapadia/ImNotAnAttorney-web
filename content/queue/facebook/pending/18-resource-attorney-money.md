### Post Content

Nobody wants to talk about this. But you need to hear it.

How your attorney gets paid directly shapes how they handle your case.

Here is the uncomfortable truth about criminal defense economics:

PUBLIC DEFENDERS: The ABA recommends a maximum of 150 felony cases per attorney per year. Many public defenders carry 300-500 active cases. If you do the math on a 50-hour work week divided by 400 clients, each client gets roughly 7.5 minutes of their attorney's time per week. That is not enough time to read a police report, let alone prepare a suppression motion.

PRIVATE ATTORNEYS (FLAT FEE): Once you pay the retainer, the economic incentive flips. Before you hired them, every minute was an investment in landing you as a client. After you hired them, every minute spent on your case is time they could spend acquiring a new client. The pressure is toward early resolution — which in criminal defense usually means a plea deal.

PRIVATE ATTORNEYS (HOURLY): The incentive is toward maximum time spent. Every meeting, every phone call, every continuance generates billing hours.

None of this means your attorney is corrupt. But it means the economic pressure they face and the outcome you need are not always the same thing.

The question to ask yourself at every stage: what is the financially optimal outcome for my attorney — and does it match what is optimal for me?

If your attorney is on a flat fee and recommending a quick plea before filing any motions or reviewing all discovery... connect the dots.

Understanding the economics doesn't mean your attorney is bad. It means you should be the one driving decisions — with full information — not being gently steered.

(This is information, not legal advice.)

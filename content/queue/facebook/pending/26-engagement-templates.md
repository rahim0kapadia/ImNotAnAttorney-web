# Engagement Comment Templates

These are templates for responding to common posts and questions in Facebook support groups. Adapt the tone to match the specific group culture. Always lead with empathy, provide information (never advice), and close with a reminder that you're sharing information, not legal counsel.

**Rules:**
- Never pitch INNA products in comments. Ever.
- Never say "you should do X." Say "here are questions you might want to ask your attorney."
- Never diagnose someone's legal situation.
- Always include the UPL disclaimer in some form.
- Be a human being first. Information source second.

---

## TEMPLATE 1: "My lawyer won't call me back"

**Trigger:** Someone posts about their attorney not returning calls for weeks or months.

**Response:**

This is incredibly frustrating, and you're not alone — it's actually the #1 bar complaint in every state, every year. Attorneys not returning calls.

A few things that might help:

First, switch from phone calls to email. Not because email is better — but because email creates a paper trail. Phone calls don't.

Send a direct email with specific questions:
- "What is the current status of my case?"
- "Have you received all discovery from the prosecution?"
- "Have any motions been filed?"
- "What is the next court date and what should I expect?"
- "When can we schedule a phone call this week?"

Give them 5 business days to respond. Document the date you sent it.

If they still don't respond, you have options — including filing a complaint with your state bar's disciplinary body. The attorney is required to respond to that.

You're not being difficult by expecting communication. You're exercising a right.

(Sharing this as general information, not legal advice — I'm not an attorney.)

## TEMPLATE 2: "Is this plea deal fair?"

**Trigger:** Someone posts asking whether they should accept a plea deal offer.

**Response:**

I can't tell you whether to take it — nobody can except you, with the guidance of your attorney. But I can share some questions that experienced defense attorneys say should be answered BEFORE any plea decision:

1. Has your attorney received and reviewed ALL discovery?
2. Can your attorney name the 3 biggest weaknesses in the prosecution's case?
3. Have any motions been filed (suppression, dismissal, etc.)?
4. What is your REALISTIC sentence exposure if you go to trial — not the maximum, the realistic range given your circumstances?
5. What are the collateral consequences of the plea? (employment, housing, custody, immigration, voting)
6. Are you eligible for diversion or deferred adjudication?
7. Can your attorney counter-offer with different terms?

If your attorney hasn't answered these questions — or can't — the plea recommendation may have come before the investigation. A plea recommendation should come AFTER the work, not before it.

The "take it by Friday" urgency is a negotiation tactic. Take the time you need to make an informed decision.

(This is information, not legal advice.)

## TEMPLATE 3: "How do I find a good attorney?"

**Trigger:** Someone asks for attorney recommendations or how to evaluate attorneys.

**Response:**

This is one of the most important decisions you'll make. A few things to consider during consultations:

Ask these questions before signing any retainer:
- "Who specifically will handle my case day-to-day? Will it be you or an associate?"
- "How many active cases are you currently handling?"
- "What motions do you anticipate filing in my case?"
- "How often will you communicate updates, even when nothing has changed?"
- "What is your approach — are you a negotiator or do you try cases?"
- "How do you bill — flat fee, hourly, or hybrid? What does trial cost on top of retainer?"

Watch for red flags:
- They promise a specific outcome during the consultation
- They spend more time talking about themselves than asking about your case
- They can't explain what work they'd actually do
- They pressure you to sign immediately

A consultation where you leave with more questions than answers is actually a good sign — it means the attorney is being honest about the complexity. Beware the one who guarantees everything in 20 minutes.

Also: check your state bar's website for disciplinary records before hiring anyone. It takes 2 minutes and can save you thousands.

(Sharing this as general information, not legal advice.)

## TEMPLATE 4: "I think my attorney isn't doing anything"

**Trigger:** Someone posts suspecting their attorney isn't working their case.

**Response:**

There's a way to find out for sure.

Most jurisdictions have online court dockets. Search "[your county] clerk of court" and look up your case number (it's on your bail paperwork or summons). You can see every document that has been filed in your case.

If the only things on the docket are appearance entries and continuance requests — no motions, no discovery demands, no defense filings — that tells you something.

Continuances are not the same as motions. A continuance just pushes your case back. A motion actually challenges the prosecution's evidence.

If you see activity that you didn't know about, that might mean your attorney is working but terrible at communicating. If you see nothing... that's a different conversation.

Either way, checking the docket gives you facts instead of feelings. And facts are what you bring to your next meeting with your attorney.

(This is general information, not legal advice.)

## TEMPLATE 5: "My public defender doesn't care about my case"

**Trigger:** Someone posts about frustration with their public defender's lack of attention.

**Response:**

I hear you, and this frustration is real.

Here's some context that might help: the American Bar Association recommends a maximum of 150 felony cases per attorney per year. Many public defenders carry 300 to 500 active cases at the same time. If you do the math on a 50-hour work week divided by 400 clients, each client gets roughly 7.5 minutes per week.

That's not a defense of the system — it's broken. But it helps explain why your public defender seems checked out. They may genuinely care about your case and simply not have the time to show it.

What you can do:
- Be the client who makes it easy. Send clear, written questions by email or letter (not phone calls).
- Ask specific questions they can answer quickly: "Have you received discovery?" "Have you considered any motions?" "What's the next step?"
- Don't ask "what's happening with my case?" — that's easy to dodge. Ask concrete questions.
- Document all communication attempts.

You can also request a substitution of counsel by filing a motion with the court, though the judge will need to find that your reasons are sufficient.

Being an engaged, prepared client — even with an overworked public defender — changes the dynamic. You become the case they actually look at.

(This is information, not legal advice.)

## TEMPLATE 6: "How do I help my son/daughter/partner who is locked up?"

**Trigger:** A family member asks how they can help their incarcerated loved one.

**Response:**

You are not helpless in this. There is real, tangible work you can do from the outside.

Things family members can do:
- Request a copy of the discovery from the attorney (you may need a signed authorization from the defendant)
- Check the court docket online — see what's been filed and what hasn't
- Keep a log of all communication attempts with the attorney — dates, times, responses
- Research your loved one's specific charges and what motions are typically filed for those charges
- Gather character letters, employment records, treatment documentation — things that can help at sentencing or plea negotiations
- Attend court dates when possible — judges notice family support

Questions to bring to the attorney on your loved one's behalf:
- "What is the current status of discovery?"
- "What motions have been filed?"
- "What is the defense strategy?"
- "What are the weaknesses in the prosecution's case?"
- "What options exist beyond a standard plea deal — diversion, deferred adjudication, charge reduction?"

Your research doesn't replace the attorney. But it can prompt the attorney to do work that might not happen otherwise.

(Sharing this as information, not legal advice.)

## TEMPLATE 7: "Can charges actually be dropped?"

**Trigger:** Someone asks if it's possible to get charges dropped or dismissed.

**Response:**

Yes, charges can be dropped or dismissed. It happens more often than most people think.

Common reasons charges get dropped or dismissed:
- Insufficient evidence — the prosecution can't prove the case beyond a reasonable doubt
- Evidence suppression — if evidence was obtained illegally (bad search, bad stop, rights violations), it can be excluded. Sometimes the remaining evidence isn't enough.
- Witness issues — a key witness recants, becomes unavailable, or has credibility problems
- Procedural errors — the prosecution missed a deadline, violated your rights, or made a legal error
- Grand jury declines to indict (in states that use grand juries)
- Diversion completion — you complete a program and charges are dismissed

The key question: has your attorney identified any grounds for dismissal or suppression in your case? Have they filed any motions?

If your attorney hasn't looked for reasons to get the charges dropped or reduced — and just started talking plea deal — that's worth a conversation.

Not every case can be dismissed. But every case should be EVALUATED for dismissal before a plea is discussed.

(This is information, not legal advice.)

## TEMPLATE 8: "My attorney wants me to take the plea but I want to go to trial"

**Trigger:** Someone is in conflict with their attorney about plea vs. trial.

**Response:**

This is one of the hardest moments in any criminal case. A few things to consider:

First — the decision to plead or go to trial is YOURS. Not your attorney's. They advise. You decide. That's the law.

But before you make that decision, you need information — not just instinct.

Ask your attorney to explain specifically:
- "Why do you recommend the plea over trial?"
- "What is my realistic sentence exposure at trial — not the maximum?"
- "What are the weaknesses in the prosecution's case that we could exploit at trial?"
- "What motions could we file that might change the landscape?"
- "What percentage of cases like mine that go to trial result in conviction?"

If the answers are: "the evidence is overwhelming, here's specifically why, and I've done the work to determine this" — that's an attorney who has earned the recommendation.

If the answers are: "you don't want to do that" or "trust me" or "that's just how it works" — that's an attorney who hasn't done the analysis.

Sometimes going to trial is the right call. Sometimes the plea is genuinely the better option. But the decision should be yours, based on specific information, not based on your attorney's convenience.

(This is information, not legal advice.)

## TEMPLATE 9: "How long should my case be taking?"

**Trigger:** Someone asks why their case is taking so long or what's normal.

**Response:**

Every case is different, but here are general timelines for reference:

- Arrest to initial appearance: 24-48 hours
- Arraignment: 2-4 weeks after arrest
- Discovery received: 2-8 weeks after arraignment
- Pre-trial motions: 4-12 weeks
- Plea negotiations: Months 2-6+
- Trial (if needed): 6-12+ months after arraignment

Continuances (delays) are extremely common and can extend these timelines significantly.

The question is not just how long it's taking — it's whether anything is happening during that time.

Ask your attorney:
- "What work has been completed since the last court date?"
- "What specific work will be done before the next court date?"
- "Is this continuance for strategic reasons or scheduling reasons?"

A case that takes 8 months because motions are being filed, discovery is being reviewed, and investigations are happening — that's fine. A case that takes 8 months with nothing on the docket but continuances — that's a problem.

Check your court docket to see for yourself what's been filed. It's public record.

(This is information, not legal advice.)

## TEMPLATE 10: "I can't afford a lawyer — what are my options?"

**Trigger:** Someone posts about not being able to afford a private attorney.

**Response:**

You have options, and you have rights.

If you cannot afford a private attorney:

1. **Public defender:** If you qualify financially, the court will appoint one. This is a constitutional right (6th Amendment). You typically request this at your first court appearance. Income qualifications vary by state.

2. **Legal aid organizations:** Many areas have nonprofit legal aid that provides free or low-cost criminal defense. Search "[your city/county] legal aid criminal defense."

3. **Law school clinics:** Law schools often have criminal defense clinics where supervised students handle real cases. Quality can be surprisingly high.

4. **Payment plans:** Some private attorneys offer payment plans or reduced fees based on circumstances. It costs nothing to ask.

If you do get a public defender, here's the honest picture: they're real attorneys, often passionate, but typically carrying 300-500 cases. You can improve your outcome by being an engaged client:

- Ask specific questions in writing
- Request and review your discovery
- Check your court docket
- Document all communication
- Bring questions to every meeting

An overworked attorney who sees that you're engaged and prepared is more likely to invest additional time in your case. You become the one they actually think about.

(This is information, not legal advice.)

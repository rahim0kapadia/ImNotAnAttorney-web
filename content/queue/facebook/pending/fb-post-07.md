## Facebook Post: What 'Not Guilty' Actually Means

### Post Content

When your attorney says "not guilty" at your arraignment, it does NOT mean you are claiming you did not do it.

This confuses almost everyone. So let me explain it in plain English.

"Not guilty" at arraignment means: "I am not admitting to anything. The prosecution needs to prove their case."

That is all. It is a legal position, not a factual claim. It preserves every option you have: trial, motions, plea negotiations, dismissal, diversion. Everything stays on the table.

If you plead "guilty" at arraignment, all of those options evaporate. You have given up your right to challenge the evidence, question the witnesses, file motions, and negotiate from a position of strength.

Almost every attorney in the country will enter a "not guilty" plea at arraignment. This is standard procedure. It does not mean your attorney thinks you are innocent. It does not mean you are lying. It means your attorney is keeping your options open.

Here is what matters more than the plea: the questions you ask AFTER the arraignment.

"When will you receive discovery?"
"What are the immediate deadlines?"
"What is our initial strategy?"
"When will I hear from you next?"

That last one, set the communication expectation now. Not after two months of silence.

(This is information, not legal advice. Court procedures vary by state.)

### Engagement Strategy

This post will surface two groups: people with upcoming arraignments who are terrified and people who already went through it and wish they had known this. Engage both. For pre-arraignment: "The five-minute version is: you show up, charges are read, attorney says not guilty, next date is set, you leave. That's it." For post-arraignment: "You're not alone in that confusion. Most people feel the same way."

### UPL Compliance Note

This post explains a universally standard legal procedure. It does not advise anyone to plead a specific way. If someone asks "should I plead not guilty?" respond with: "Your attorney will recommend the appropriate plea based on your situation. In the overwhelming majority of cases, 'not guilty' at arraignment is standard procedure, but that's a conversation for you and your attorney."

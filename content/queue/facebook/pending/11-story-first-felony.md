### Post Content

She was 34 years old. Two kids. A job she liked. A little apartment she was proud of.

Then one Tuesday night, she got pulled over for a broken tail light.

The officer said he smelled marijuana. She said it must have been from a friend who was in the car earlier. He asked to search the car. She didn't know she could say no.

They found a small amount in the center console. Not hers. But it was her car. They charged her with possession.

She had never been arrested before. She had never been inside a courtroom. She Googled her charges that night and read sentences measured in years. She couldn't eat. She couldn't sleep. She kept looking at her kids and thinking: what happens to them?

Her court-appointed attorney met her for the first time at arraignment. In the hallway. For four minutes. He told her to plead not guilty — standard — and that he would be in touch.

He wasn't. Not for six weeks.

She called. She emailed. She left voicemails. Nothing. Her second court date came and she found out he had filed a continuance without telling her.

Nobody was explaining anything. Nobody was telling her what was happening. She felt like she was watching her own life through a window and couldn't get in.

Then her sister found an article online about discovery rights. She learned she could ask to see the evidence. She learned she could look up her court docket. She learned she could ask specific questions and expect specific answers.

She wrote an email to her attorney with five direct questions: Have you received discovery? Have you filed any motions? What is your strategy? What are the weaknesses in the case? When can we meet?

He called her back the next day. First time in weeks.

When they met, she brought her list. He seemed surprised that she knew what to ask. They actually talked about her case — for real, for the first time.

Turns out the field test on the substance was never confirmed by a lab test. The officer's report had inconsistencies about the timeline of the stop. Her attorney filed a motion to suppress. The charges were eventually reduced to a misdemeanor. She completed a diversion program. Her record was cleared.

She almost pled to a felony. She almost lost her apartment and her job and her custody arrangement. Because nobody told her she had the right to ask questions.

That is why information matters.

Not legal advice. Just information. The kind that changes outcomes.

## Facebook Post: Have You Read Your Discovery?

**Type**: Text post (colored background)

---

### Post Content

Genuine question for this group:

Has anyone here actually sat down and read their own discovery documents?

Not asked your attorney about them. Not heard a summary. Actually held the pages in your hands and read them yourself.

Because here is something most defendants never hear: you know your own life better than your attorney does. You were there. You know the people. You know the timeline. You know what happened and what did not happen.

When you read your own discovery, you might notice things your attorney missed:
- A wrong date on a police report
- A description that does not match what actually happened
- Two officers telling different versions of the same event
- A witness who was never actually there
- A timeline that does not add up

Your attorney has dozens or hundreds of other cases. You have one. Yours.

If you have not requested a copy of your discovery, you can. It is your right. Ask your attorney: "Can I have a copy of the full discovery to review myself?"

Have you read yours? What did you find?

(This is information, not legal advice.)

### Visual Direction

Colored text background, dark charcoal or deep gray. The genuine question tone and "you know your life better than your attorney" reframe create engagement. No image.

### Link Strategy

No link in post. If someone asks how to read discovery or what to look for, reply with a link to "How to Read Your Discovery" blog post in a comment.

### Engagement Strategy

This post generates two types of comments: (1) people who read their discovery and found something, and (2) people who did not know they could ask for it. Respond to the first group with "That's exactly the kind of thing that matters, did you bring it to your attorney?" Respond to the second with "You absolutely can request a copy. Ask your attorney this week."

### UPL Compliance Note

If someone shares specific details from their discovery in the comments, do NOT analyze it or tell them what it means legally. Say: "That sounds like something worth bringing to your attorney's attention. A good question to ask might be: 'What does this inconsistency mean for my case?'"

### Post Content

POLL: Have you ever looked up your own case on the court docket?

Most counties let you search court records online for free. You can see every document filed in your case, every motion, every continuance, every order.

A) Yes, I check it regularly
B) Yes, I checked once
C) I didn't know I could do that
D) I tried but couldn't figure out how
E) My attorney told me not to worry about it

---

If you voted C or D: You can do this right now. Search "[your county] clerk of court" on Google. Look for "case search" or "court records." Enter your case number (it's on your bail paperwork, summons, or any court document). You will see every filing.

If you voted E: "Don't worry about it" is not a satisfying answer when your freedom is on the line. Your case file is public record. You have every right to look at it.

What matters once you see the docket: count the motions vs. the continuances. Motions are how your attorney challenges the prosecution's case. Continuances are how your attorney delays it. Both have a place. But a docket with zero motions and four continuances tells a specific story.

What did you find when you checked?

(This is information, not legal advice.)

### Link Strategy

No link in the post. If the comment thread generates requests for help finding dockets, respond with general instructions: "Search '[county name] clerk of court' and look for a case search page. Most states have this." Do NOT link to specific court websites.

### Engagement Strategy

This poll has a built-in action step: people who vote C or D can immediately go check their docket and come back to report what they found. Encourage this: "If you just checked for the first time, come back and tell us what you see. How many motions? How many continuances?"

The follow-up comments from people checking their dockets for the first time will be the most powerful content in the thread. Engage with each one.

After 24-48 hours, post a follow-up: "X% of people in this group didn't know they could check their own court docket. That's not a personal failing, it's a system that doesn't want you to look."

### UPL Compliance Note

Court dockets are public record. Encouraging someone to look at their own public court record is not legal advice. The continuance vs. motion distinction is factual, not advisory. If someone shares their docket contents and asks "is this bad?" respond with: "The docket shows you what has been filed. Whether those filings are appropriate for your case is a conversation for your attorney. But now you have the facts to have that conversation."

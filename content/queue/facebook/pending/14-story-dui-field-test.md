### Post Content

The field test said positive. His attorney said: "The evidence is clear."

But the lab test told a different story.

He was pulled over during a traffic stop. The officer found a small bag in his jacket pocket. White powder. The officer ran a field test right there on the hood of the patrol car. The test turned a color. The officer said: "That's positive."

He was arrested. Charged with possession. His attorney reviewed the police report, saw "field test positive," and started talking plea deal.

Here is what most people don't know: roadside field tests are notoriously unreliable. They have false positive rates that would shock you. Common substances — caffeine, certain medications, baking soda, even some vitamins — can trigger a positive result on cheap field test kits.

That is why there is a lab test. The field test gives officers probable cause for an arrest. The lab test is the actual evidence.

In his case, nobody checked whether the lab test had been completed. Not the officer. Not the prosecution. Not his attorney.

He learned about the difference between field tests and lab tests from a post in a support group. He asked his attorney: "Has the lab test been completed? What were the results?"

His attorney checked. The lab results had come back three weeks earlier. The substance was not what the field test said it was. It was not an illegal substance.

The charges were dropped.

Dropped.

He almost pled guilty to possession of a substance that was never illegal. Because the field test said "positive" and nobody bothered to check the lab.

Two questions every person facing drug charges should ask their attorney:

1. "Has the substance been tested in a laboratory — not just a field test?"
2. "What were the actual lab results, and do they match the charges?"

A field test is not evidence. It is a starting point. Don't let your case end where it began.

(This story is a composite based on common experiences. It is not about a specific individual. This is information, not legal advice.)

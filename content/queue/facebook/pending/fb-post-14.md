### Post Content

The police report said he consented to the search.

The body camera showed something different.

He was pulled over for a wide turn. According to the police report, the officer asked if he could search the vehicle and the defendant said "sure, go ahead." Standard language. Clean consent. No Fourth Amendment issue. Case closed.

His attorney read the report and said: "They had consent. Not much we can do there."

But his brother, who had been reading about defense strategies, asked one question that changed the trajectory of the case:

"Is there body camera footage? And does it match the report?"

The attorney requested the body camera footage as part of discovery. It took four weeks. When it arrived, they watched it together.

The footage showed the officer saying: "I'm going to take a look in your car." Not a question. A statement. At no point on the recording did the defendant say "sure, go ahead" or anything close to it. The defendant was standing against the patrol car. He did not verbally consent. He did not nod. He did not gesture toward the vehicle. The officer opened the door and searched.

The report said consent. The camera said otherwise.

The attorney filed a motion to suppress the evidence found during the search. The argument: there was no voluntary consent, and the officer's report did not match the objective record.

The motion was granted. The evidence was suppressed. The case collapsed.

Three things to take from this:

1. Police reports are written by the officer. Body cameras record what actually happened. When these two sources conflict, it matters.

2. Body camera footage is discoverable. Your attorney can and should request it. If they have not, ask: "Have you requested all available body camera and dashcam footage?"

3. If the footage shows something different from the report, your attorney should be filing a motion. That inconsistency is the kind of thing that wins motions.

Ask your attorney:

"Is there body camera or dashcam footage in my case?"
"Have you reviewed it?"
"Does it match the police report?"

These are not aggressive questions. They are the questions a thorough attorney asks automatically.

(This story is a composite based on common experiences. It is not about a specific individual. This is information, not legal advice.)

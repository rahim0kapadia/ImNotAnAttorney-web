### Post Content

There are two words that show up on your court docket, and most defendants do not know the difference between them.

CONTINUANCE: Your attorney asked to push your court date back. That is it. Your case got postponed. No work was necessarily done. No evidence was challenged. The date just moved.

MOTION: Your attorney asked the court to DO something. Suppress evidence. Compel the prosecution to turn over documents. Dismiss a charge. Reconsider bail. A motion is how your attorney actually fights your case.

Here is the problem: many defendants see multiple entries on their court docket and assume things are happening. But if you look closely and every single entry is a continuance or an appearance filing, nothing has happened. Your case has been postponed, not defended.

Continuances are sometimes strategic. Waiting for lab results. Scheduling an expert witness. Buying time for a related ruling. If your attorney can explain WHY they requested the continuance and WHAT work was completed during the delay, that is fine.

But if every continuance comes with: "We're still working on it" or "I needed more time", ask the follow-up: "More time to do what, specifically?"

A continuance without purpose is not a defense strategy. It is a delay.

Check your docket. Count the continuances. Count the motions. That ratio tells you something.

(This is information, not legal advice.)

### Engagement Strategy

Respond to comments where people share their continuance count. If someone says "I've had 6 continuances and zero motions," respond with empathy: "That ratio is worth a conversation with your attorney. A good question to ask: 'What specific work will be completed before the next court date?'"

### UPL Compliance Note

Do NOT tell anyone whether their continuance count is too high or whether their attorney is performing. Say: "That ratio is something to discuss with your attorney" or "Consider asking what work was done during each delay."

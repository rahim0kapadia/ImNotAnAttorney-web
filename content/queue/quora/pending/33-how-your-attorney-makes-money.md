## Quora: INNA-Q33 | How do criminal defense attorneys get paid, and does it affect how they handle my case?
**Topics**: Criminal Defense, Attorney Fees, Retainer, Public Defender, Legal Costs
**SEO**: "how criminal defense attorneys get paid" / "criminal lawyer fee structure" / "retainer fee criminal defense"

Nobody wants to think about this. You hired someone to defend your freedom and the idea that money shapes how they handle your case feels uncomfortable. But it's real, and understanding it protects you.

**Public defenders** are paid by the government. The funding problem is well documented. The ABA recommends a maximum of 150 felony cases per attorney per year. Most public defender offices run 300-500 active cases per attorney. At 400 cases and a 50-hour work week, each client gets roughly 7.5 minutes of attorney time per week. Many public defenders are skilled and passionate, the problem isn't the people, it's the math.

**Private attorneys typically charge a flat fee retainer.** $3,000-$10,000 for a misdemeanor. $10,000-$50,000+ for a felony. Trial, investigators, and expert witnesses usually cost extra.

Here's what most defendants don't understand: once you pay the retainer, the incentive flips. Before they signed you, every hour they spent on marketing was an investment in landing the client. After they sign you, every hour they spend on your case is time they can't bill to someone new. A quick plea deal is profitable. A three-month suppression battle followed by trial is expensive.

This doesn't mean your attorney is deliberately selling you out. It means the financial pressure toward a plea deal is built into the business model, and you should be the one deciding whether a plea is right for you, with full information about alternatives.

**Hourly billing** has the opposite incentive problem: more time means more revenue. This can mean thoroughness, but it can also mean inefficiency and padding.

**The "free consultation" is a sales call.** When you walk in for that first meeting, the attorney is evaluating you as a potential client. Can this person pay? Is this case manageable? That's not cynicism, it's how every professional service works. But you should enter every consultation knowing it's a revenue evaluation, not a legal analysis.

**The #1 bar complaint nationally is attorneys not returning client calls.** Not malpractice. Not incompetence. Communication failures. And it makes sense when you look at the math: an attorney with 200 cases spending 15 minutes per client call per week would spend 50 hours just on calls, their entire work week, before doing any legal work.

**How to protect yourself:**

Ask specific questions instead of "what's the status?" Ask: "Have you filed the motion to suppress?" "Have you reviewed all discovery?" "What motions are you planning to file?" Specific questions are harder to dodge than general ones.

Put every request in writing. Email creates a paper trail.

Know what billing structure you're under, and ask yourself: at every stage of my case, what is the financially optimal outcome for my attorney, and does that match what's optimal for me?

The best attorneys are the ones where the answer is yes. But you can't evaluate that if you don't understand the economics.

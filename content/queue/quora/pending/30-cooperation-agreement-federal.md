## Quora: INNA-Q30 | What does it mean to cooperate in a federal criminal case? Is it worth it?
**Topics**: Federal Cooperation, Proffer Agreement, Substantial Assistance, Federal Sentencing, Criminal Defense
**SEO**: "federal cooperation agreement" / "proffer queen for a day" / "substantial assistance sentencing"

Cooperation in a federal case is nothing like what most people imagine. It's not "tell us what you know and we'll go easy on you." It's a formalized legal process with strict rules, significant risks, and no guarantee of the outcome you're hoping for.

**The proffer session** is the first step. You and your attorney sit with prosecutors and agents. You tell them what you know. Both sides sign a proffer agreement first -- the "queen for a day" protection.

Here's what that agreement actually protects: the government generally can't use your words directly against you at trial. That's it.

What it does NOT protect:

The government can use your information to find other evidence. If you mention a hidden bank account, they can't play back your recording -- but they can go find the account and subpoena the records. That's called derivative use, and it's fair game.

If you later testify at trial and contradict anything you said during the proffer, the government can use your proffer statements to shred your credibility. This means you're locked into whatever story you told them. Deviate from it and they'll destroy you on the stand.

If the government determines you lied during the proffer -- about anything, even something minor -- the agreement is void. Every word you said becomes admissible evidence against you. Every admission. Every detail.

**The cooperation agreement** comes after the proffer if the government wants your continued help. You agree to provide complete and truthful information about all criminal activity you're aware of, testify at trials and grand jury proceedings, produce documents, and potentially participate in undercover operations. In return, the government files a 5K1.1 substantial assistance motion at sentencing, asking the judge to go below the guidelines.

**The risk nobody talks about:** buried in every cooperation agreement is language saying the government files that 5K1.1 motion "at its sole discretion." They decide if your help was good enough. Not you. Not your attorney. Not the judge. You've already pled guilty. You've already admitted everything. And if they decide your cooperation wasn't "substantial," you're sentenced at full guidelines with no credit.

This is the fundamental asymmetry of federal cooperation: you give up everything upfront, and the benefit comes later -- if the government decides you earned it.

**Questions before you agree to anything:**

- What information does the government already have? (If they know most of it, your cooperation has less value.)
- What's the worst case if the proffer goes badly?
- Can we do a reverse proffer first -- where the government shows us their evidence before we provide anything?
- What does "satisfactory cooperation" mean in the specific terms of this agreement?
- What's the realistic sentencing range with cooperation vs. without?

Cooperation can be the smartest move in a federal case. It can also make things dramatically worse. The difference is usually whether the defendant went in with eyes open or got talked into it by a prosecutor's pitch.

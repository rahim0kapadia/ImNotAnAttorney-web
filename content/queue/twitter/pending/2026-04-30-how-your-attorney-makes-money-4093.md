How your attorney gets paid directly shapes how they handle your case.

Not because most attorneys are bad people. Because the business model of criminal defense creates pressures defendants rarely see.

Here's what those pressures actually are:
===TWEET===
The public defender math:

ABA recommends max 150 felony cases per attorney per year. Most PD offices carry 300-500 simultaneously. At 400 cases / 50-hour week: each client gets ~7. 5 minutes of attorney time per week. Not enough to read a police report.
===TWEET===
Legally required to represent you. Not funded to do it well.
===TWEET===
The private attorney fee problem:

Before you hired them: every hour in your consultation was an investment in landing the engagement. After you hired them: every hour they spend on your case is money they can't bill to a new client.
===TWEET===
This creates quiet pressure toward early resolution. A quick plea is profitable. A three-month suppression battle is not.
===TWEET===
The flat fee structure:

Most criminal defense is done on flat fees. Once the flat fee is paid, additional work costs the attorney without additional revenue. The incentive is toward minimum necessary effort.
===TWEET===
Motion work, trial prep, independent investigation — all cost them without paying them. Know what your retainer actually covers.
===TWEET===
The free consultation reality:

It's a sales call, not legal advice. Research on attorney business development is explicit: the consultation is a revenue evaluation — whether you can pay, whether the case is manageable, what's the minimum needed to close the deal.
===TWEET===
Questions to evaluate a consultation:
• Did they ask about your budget or about your case? • Did they give a realistic range, or just what you want to hear?
===TWEET===
What good alignment actually looks like:

✓ They present the plea offer AND the case for trial — and let you decide
✓ They file motions even when it costs them unbilled time
✓ They share discovery with you and explain it

===TWEET===
It's the bar for adequate.

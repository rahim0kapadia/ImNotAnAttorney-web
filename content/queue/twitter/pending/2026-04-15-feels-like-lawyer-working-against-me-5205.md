"Whose side are you on?"

This feeling — that your own attorney is working against you — is one of the most common things defendants tell us.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: sometimes you're right.

Here's how to tell which it is:
===TWEET===
Why it happens (it's not always sinister):

1. They took too many cases. Your plea deal is faster than your defense. 2. They've already decided you're guilty. They're doing damage control, not defense. 3. They're afraid of the judge or prosecutor.
===TWEET===
They value that relationship more than your rights. 4. It's actually a communication failure. The evidence is bad and they don't know how to say it.
===TWEET===
Warning sign #1: They agreed to terms without consulting you. This is the biggest one. If your attorney agreed to ANYTHING — a continuance, a plea framework, a sentencing recommendation — without discussing it with you first, that's a violation of their duty.
===TWEET===
YOU make the decisions. They advise.
===TWEET===
Warning sign #2: "Take this deal before they take it off the table."

Urgency can be real. But manufactured urgency to close out a case fast?

That's an attorney managing their schedule, not your defense.

The prosecution's "deadline" is almost never as hard as presented.
===TWEET===
Warning sign #3: More time with the prosecutor than with you.

Professional relationships between attorneys are normal.

But if your lawyer is laughing and chatting with opposing counsel while you sit there terrified — that dynamic is broken.
===TWEET===
What to do:

1. Ask direct questions: "What is your theory of defense? " "Why aren't we filing a suppression motion? "
2. Put your concerns in writing (creates a paper trail AND forces a written response)
3. Check the court docket — are there any defense filings at all?
===TWEET===
Write down the answers. Patterns matter.
===TWEET===

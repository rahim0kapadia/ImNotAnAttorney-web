"You blew a .09."

That number came from a machine.

A machine that needs calibration. That was operated by someone who needs certification. That sat in a patrol car through summer heat.

The BAC result isn't a fact. It's a data point. And data points can be wrong.
===TWEET===
Challenge Point 1: Calibration Records

Was the device calibrated on schedule? Did it actually pass? What reference solution was used? Who performed it? A breathalyzer that's overdue for calibration doesn't produce reliable science, it produces a number.
===TWEET===
Those aren't the same thing.
===TWEET===
Challenge Point 2: Operator Certification

Not "certified in breathalyzer operation" generally. Certified on the specific make and model used in your test. An Intoxilyzer 8000 is not a Draeger 9510.
===TWEET===
If the certification lapsed before your test date, that's an uncertified operator running the result.
===TWEET===
Challenge Point 3: The Observation Period

Before the test, the officer is supposed to watch you continuously for 15-20 minutes. No eating, drinking, burping. Most "observation periods" consist of an officer checking paperwork while you sit nearby.
===TWEET===
And the form still gets checked: "observation period completed. "
===TWEET===
Challenge Point 4: Error Margin

Most breath testing devices have an accepted margin of error of ±0.005 to ±0.02 BAC.

A reading of .08 could actually be anywhere from .06 to .10.

For someone who blew .08 or .09, that margin is the entire case.
===TWEET===
If your attorney is treating the BAC number like a carved-in-stone fact, they're conceding the prosecution's strongest evidence without a fight.

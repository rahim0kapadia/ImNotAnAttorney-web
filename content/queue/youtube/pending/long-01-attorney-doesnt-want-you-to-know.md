**[1:15-3:00] SECTION 1: HOW PRIVATE ATTORNEYS GET PAID**
[Text on screen: "THE RETAINER MODEL"]
[B-roll: person signing paperwork, writing a check]
"Most private criminal defense attorneys charge a flat retainer. Misdemeanor: three to ten thousand dollars. Felony: ten to fifty thousand or more. Trial costs extra. Experts cost extra. Investigators cost extra."

"There are three billing models, and each one creates different incentives."

[Text on screen: "Flat fee"]
"Flat fee. You pay one amount for the whole case. The alignment problem: once that flat fee is paid, every additional hour of work costs the attorney without generating additional revenue. The incentive is toward minimum necessary effort."

[Text on screen: "Hourly"]
"Hourly. You pay for time spent. The alignment problem: the incentive is toward maximum time spent. Inefficiency. Drawn-out processes. Padding."

[Text on screen: "Hybrid"]
"Hybrid. Flat fee for standard work, hourly for trial. This is the most common structure in serious criminal defense. The alignment problem: this creates a strong incentive to resolve cases before the hourly clock starts. Translation: settle before trial."

"None of these structures is inherently corrupt. But each one creates pressures you should understand."

---

**[3:00-4:30] SECTION 2: THE INCENTIVE FLIP**
[PATTERN INTERRUPT: screen goes black for 1 second, then text appears: "THE FLIP"]
[Direct to camera, closer framing]
"This is the part nobody explains."

"Before you hire a private attorney, every hour they spend on your case, that initial consultation, the follow-up call, reviewing your arrest report, is an investment in landing the engagement. They're earning your business."

"After you hire them? After you've signed the retainer and the check has cleared? Every hour they spend on your case is an hour they can't spend acquiring or serving a new client."

"The economic incentive flips. Completely."

[Text on screen: "A quick plea = profitable. A 3-month suppression fight = expensive."]
"A quick plea deal is profitable. It closes the file, frees the calendar, and opens space for the next retainer. A three-month suppression battle followed by a two-week trial? That's expensive to staff, risky to the attorney's win rate, and may not generate one additional dollar."

"This does NOT mean your attorney will deliberately sell you out. Most won't. But the pressure toward early resolution, toward the plea, is built into the math. And if you don't understand the math, you can't evaluate whether the plea recommendation is in YOUR best interest or in your attorney's best interest."

---

**[4:30-6:15] SECTION 3: THE PUBLIC DEFENDER MATH**
[PATTERN INTERRUPT: on-screen graphic showing numbers]
[Text on screen: "ABA recommendation: 150 felony cases/year"]
"Now let's talk about public defenders. Different problem. Same result."

"The American Bar Association recommends a maximum of 150 felony cases per attorney per year. In practice? Many public defenders carry 300 to 500 active cases at the same time."

[Text on screen: "400 cases / 50 hours per week = 7.5 minutes per client"]
"Let's do the math. If a public defender works a 50-hour week, which is generous, and divides it by 400 clients, each client gets approximately seven and a half minutes of their attorney's time per week. That's not enough time to read a single police report, let alone draft a suppression motion."

"This isn't a failure of individual public defenders. Many are genuinely skilled, dedicated attorneys doing extraordinary work under impossible conditions. It's a systemic, documented underfunding of a constitutional right."

"Criminal justice researchers have documented this exhaustively: innocent people plead guilty because their attorney didn't have time to investigate. Cases that should have been won are lost because motions were never filed. Not because the attorney didn't care. Because the math made it impossible."

[Direct to camera]
"Your public defender may care deeply about your case. But caring and having the time to act on that care are different things."

---

**[6:15-7:30] SECTION 4: GREET 'EM, MEET 'EM, PLEAD 'EM**
[Text on screen: "Greet 'em. Meet 'em. Plead 'em."]
"There's a phrase in the legal profession. Greet 'em, meet 'em, plead 'em. It describes a pattern where an attorney meets a client once, reviews nothing carefully, and pushes whatever plea the prosecution offers."

"Assembly-line justice. And it happens more than the profession likes to admit."

"The question isn't whether plea deals are inherently bad. Sometimes the plea is the smart move, the evidence is overwhelming, the offer is genuinely below guidelines, your attorney has done the work and concluded this is the best path."

"The question is: did the recommendation come AFTER the investigation? Or before it? Because if your attorney is recommending a plea before they've reviewed discovery, before they've filed motions, before they've investigated anything, that's not strategy. That's the path of least resistance."

---

**[7:30-8:45] SECTION 5: THE CONTINUANCE GAME**
[PATTERN INTERRUPT: calendar graphic with dates being X'd out]
"Your attorney has requested another continuance. Fourth one in a row. Each time: 'We need more time to prepare.'"

"Sometimes that's true. Continuances can be genuinely strategic. But continuances are also the primary tool attorneys use to manage overbooking."

"An attorney with too many cases can push hearings back to make room in their calendar. Defendants are told it's strategy. The real reason is scheduling."

"How do you tell the difference? Simple."

[Text on screen: "Strategic continuance = specific answer. Scheduling continuance = vague answer."]
"Ask: what specific work will be completed during this additional time? A strategic continuance comes with a specific answer. A scheduling continuance generates a vague one."

"Also: ask what's happened between the last continuance and now. If the answer is 'not much', well. That's your answer too."

---

**[8:45-10:00] SECTION 6: FREE CONSULTATIONS**
[B-roll: law office waiting room, business cards]
"One more thing. Those free consultations? Let me reframe them for you."

"A free consultation is a sales call. When you walk into an attorney's office, you're being evaluated as a potential client. The attorney is asking: can this person pay? Is this case manageable? What's the minimum I need to say to close the deal?"

"This doesn't mean every consultation is dishonest. Some attorneys are genuinely consultative from the first meeting. But you should walk in knowing what it is."

[Text on screen: "How to evaluate a consultation:"]
"Red flags in a consultation: they ask more about your budget than your case. They speak in generalities without identifying specific weaknesses in the prosecution's case. They promise everything will be fine. They describe themselves more than the work they'd actually do."

"A consultation where you leave with more questions than answers? That might actually be the honest one. Criminal defense is complex. An attorney who solves it all in twenty minutes either hasn't thought about it hard enough or isn't being straight with you."

**[10:00-12:00] SECTION 7: THE 7 QUESTIONS THAT HOLD ANY ATTORNEY ACCOUNTABLE**
[Text on screen: "THE ACCOUNTABILITY QUESTIONS"]
[Direct to camera, slower pace, deliberate]
"Here's what you came for. Seven questions. Write them down. Bring them to your next meeting. These work on private attorneys. These work on public defenders. These work on any attorney in any case."

[Each question appears as text on screen as it's spoken]

"Number one: What specific work have you done on my case since our last court date?"

"Number two: Have you received and reviewed ALL discovery?"

"Number three: What are the three biggest weaknesses in the prosecution's case?"

"Number four: What motions have you filed, or are you planning to file?"

"Number five: What is the realistic sentence exposure if we go to trial, not the maximum, the realistic number?"

"Number six: What are the collateral consequences of this plea, employment, housing, immigration, licensing?"

"Number seven: If this were your case, not your client's case, YOUR case, what would you do?"

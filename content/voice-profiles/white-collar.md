## 1. Voice Summary

The ImNotAnAttorney Team writes for white collar and federal defendants like a strategic advisor laying out a chess board. These readers are professionals, accountants, executives, business owners, physicians, engineers, who have never been inside a courtroom and never expected to be. Their terror is not shame and it is not raw fear of the streets. It is confusion. The investigation crept along for months, maybe years, and then suddenly the numbers in the indictment are numbers they cannot comprehend. They are used to understanding systems, solving problems, reading fine print. The voice meets them at that level: calm, analytical, precise. These readers can handle a higher reading level (10th grade is acceptable for this category). They do not need you to simplify, they need you to explain the system. Why federal investigations take 18 months. Why the conviction rate appears insurmountable. Why cooperation is not the obvious play everyone says it is. The voice is never panicked, never breathless, never dumbed down. It is the voice of someone who has watched this chess game play out hundreds of times and can finally show them where all the pieces are, how they move, and what most people miss.

**Word budget: 1,000-1,500 words body content. Maximum 1,800. Every sentence must teach, validate, or arm, delete anything that only fills space.**

## 3. DO/DON'T Examples

### Example 1: The federal investigation timeline (why it's slow and what that means)
- **DO:** "Federal investigations typically run 12-24 months before anyone sees an indictment (DOJ Office of the Inspector General). That's not incompetence. It's architecture. Federal prosecutors build cases the way engineers build bridges, they don't start pouring concrete until every load calculation is finished. What that means for you: the case they're bringing has been stress-tested internally for over a year. Knowing that changes what 'fighting the charges' actually looks like."
- **DON'T:** "Federal investigations take a really long time and you need to be worried because they've been building a case against you for months or even years before you ever knew about it!"

### Example 2: The 90%+ federal conviction rate (what it actually means)
- **DO:** "The federal conviction rate sits above 90% (U.S. Sentencing Commission). That number is real. But it is an aggregate that flattens critical distinctions. Roughly two-thirds of those convictions come from cooperation agreements, defendants who provided substantial assistance in exchange for reduced sentences. The trial conviction rate and the cooperation conviction rate tell very different stories. Before that number paralyzes you, the first question is: which path are you actually on?"
- **DON'T:** "The federal conviction rate is over 90%, which means the odds are stacked against you. But don't give up hope, many people have beaten federal charges and you can too if you fight hard enough."

### Example 3: Cooperation decisions (proffer / "queen for a day")
- **DO:** "A proffer session, sometimes called 'queen for a day', is a meeting where you provide information to the government under a limited use agreement. Limited, not absolute. What you say generally cannot be used directly against you in the government's case-in-chief. But it can be used to find other evidence, and it can be used for impeachment if you testify at trial and contradict your proffer. Whether cooperation makes strategic sense depends on at least 4 variables: the strength of the government's existing evidence, whether you have information the government actually needs, the sentencing exposure without cooperation, and whether other defendants are already cooperating. None of those have simple answers."
- **DON'T:** "Never talk to the feds. They will use everything you say against you. The proffer is a trap and cooperating means snitching on people."

### Example 4: Wire fraud / mail fraud charges (the catch-all)
- **DO:** "Wire fraud (18 U.S.C. SS 1343) and mail fraud (18 U.S.C. SS 1341) are the most flexible tools in the federal prosecutor's kit. If any part of the alleged scheme touched a phone, an email, a wire transfer, or the postal service, the government has a wire or mail fraud charge available. That breadth is the point, these statutes are designed to be catch-alls. Understanding what the government has to prove is more useful than being alarmed by the charge label itself. They need to establish a scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and a use of wire or mail in furtherance of that scheme. Each of those three elements has specific pressure points."
- **DON'T:** "Wire fraud is a very serious charge and carries up to 20 years in prison. If you've been charged with wire fraud you need to understand that the government has broad powers to prosecute these cases."

### Example 5: Asset forfeiture fears
- **DO:** "The forfeiture paragraph in a federal indictment lists everything the government intends to seize, bank accounts, property, vehicles, connected to the alleged criminal activity. Reading that list for the first time feels like reading the end of your financial life. It is not. Forfeiture is a negotiation, not an automatic outcome. The government must establish a connection between each listed asset and the alleged offense. The timing of forfeiture actions also matters, there are preservation steps that apply in the early stages of a case, and the window for some of those steps is narrow."
- **DON'T:** "The government can take everything you own. If you've been charged with a federal crime they can freeze your bank accounts, seize your house, and take your car. You could lose everything."

### Example 6: Professional license / career consequences
- **DO:** "For professionals, physicians, CPAs, attorneys, financial advisors, engineers, the licensing question often causes more anxiety than the criminal case itself. The reality is more granular than the fear. Professional licensing consequences vary by state, by licensing board, and by the nature of the conviction. Many professionals continue working during the pendency of a federal case, which typically spans 1-3 years (Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts). Some licensing boards distinguish between felonies and misdemeanors, some between crimes of moral turpitude and other offenses. The specific charge and disposition matter more than the fact of being charged."
- **DON'T:** "Your career is probably over. Federal charges will destroy your professional reputation and you'll likely lose your license."

### Example 7: Grand jury subpoena response
- **DO:** "A grand jury subpoena means the government wants you to testify or produce documents before a grand jury. It does not mean you are the target, grand juries hear from witnesses, subjects, and targets. The distinction matters. The subpoena itself should indicate what category you fall into, or your attorney can contact the assigned AUSA to clarify. If you are a target, the calculus around compliance shifts significantly. If you are a witness, the calculus is different but still not simple, what you say under oath before a grand jury follows you through the entire case."
- **DON'T:** "Getting a grand jury subpoena means you're in big trouble. The grand jury is the government's tool to build cases and if they're calling you in, you're probably going to be indicted."

### Example 8: "Should I talk to the agents?" scenario
- **DO:** "Two agents show up at your office. They're polite. They say they just have a few questions and you're not in trouble. Here is what 'just a few questions' looks like from the other side: the agents have prepared for this conversation. They already know the answers to most of what they're asking. They are not gathering information, they are testing whether your answers match what they already have. Inconsistencies become evidence. This is not a conversation. It is an interview being conducted by trained investigators on a recorded or documented basis. Whether to speak to federal agents is one of the most consequential early decisions in a federal case, and it is not a decision that benefits from being made in the moment at your office door."
- **DON'T:** "NEVER talk to federal agents! Anything you say will be used against you. Just say you want a lawyer and shut the door. You have rights!"

## Structure Template

Every post follows this skeleton:
1. Hook (2-3 sentences), name the fear, validate, promise the answer
2. TLDRBox, one-sentence answer + one 5-minute action
3. H2: Core answer (300-400 words), the thing they came for, with insider detail
4. H2: Actions (200-300 words), 2-3 things to do RIGHT NOW
5. H2: Common mistake (150-200 words), one error + how to avoid it
6. Agency close (2 sentences), competence, not fear
7. Product bridge (3-4 sentences), free value → paid depth for YOUR case

Total: 1,000-1,500 words. The reader finishes in 3-4 minutes on their phone.
The product bridge earns its place because the free content already delivered value.

### Terms to Define Inline (first use only)
- **Proffer agreement**, "sometimes called 'queen for a day', a meeting where you provide information to the government under a limited use agreement"
- **Target letter**, "a notification from the U.S. Attorney's office that you are the focus of a federal investigation"
- **Subject letter**, "a notification that your conduct is within the scope of a federal investigation, but you are not the primary focus"
- **Sentencing guidelines**, "the federal framework (U.S. Sentencing Guidelines) that judges use to calculate sentence ranges based on offense level and criminal history"
- **Forfeiture**, "government seizure of assets the prosecution alleges are connected to criminal activity"
- **Conspiracy**, "an agreement between two or more people to commit an offense, the most common federal charge, which does not require the crime to have been completed"
- **Substantial assistance**, "cooperation significant enough that the prosecutor files a motion allowing the judge to sentence below the guidelines range"
- **AUSA**, "Assistant United States Attorney, the federal prosecutor handling your case"
- **Superseding indictment**, "a new version of the indictment that replaces the original, often adding charges or defendants"
- **Loss amount**, "the dollar figure the government attributes to the alleged scheme, calculated using methods that often produce numbers far higher than expected"
- **Safety valve**, "a provision that allows certain defendants to be sentenced below mandatory minimums if specific criteria are met"

## 5. Opening Pattern

### The Punch (1-3 sentences)
The moment it became real. For white collar and federal defendants, this is almost always a single, specific event: the target letter on your desk, the agents in the lobby asking for you, the subpoena served at the office, the news article with your name in it, the morning you saw the forfeiture notice on your bank account. These readers have a precise "the world shifted" moment. Name it.

### The Turn (1-2 sentences)
Shift from that moment to the system behind it. "Federal investigations follow a pattern. Once you see the pattern, you can see where you are in it, and what's likely coming next."

### The Roadmap (1 sentence)
The strategic framework this post provides. No "In this comprehensive guide, we will explore..." Just: "This post maps [specific strategic framework], from [starting point] to [end state]."

### Example Opening
> The letter arrived on a Tuesday. Official DOJ letterhead. Your name. The words "target of a federal investigation." Thirty seconds ago you were planning Q3 revenue. Now you're reading a sentence that contains the phrase "wire fraud" and a statute number you've never seen before.
>
> Federal investigations follow a pattern. The letter, the timeline, the charges, none of it is random. Once you see how the pattern works, you can see exactly where you are in it.
>
> This post maps the federal investigation timeline, from the moment you learn you're a target through indictment, and the 3 decision points that matter most at each stage.

## 6. Emotional Calibration

### Handling the Conviction Rate (Witte Threat-Efficacy Pairing)
The 90%+ federal conviction rate is the single most paralyzing number these readers encounter. Never state it without decomposing it.

**Template:**
> The federal conviction rate exceeds 90% (U.S. Sentencing Commission). That number includes cases where defendants cooperated AND cases that went to trial. The math breaks down differently depending on which path you're on. Roughly two-thirds of convictions come through cooperation agreements. Of the cases that go to trial, acquittal rates vary by offense type and circuit. The number that matters is the one for defendants in your specific situation, not the aggregate.

**Pair with action within 2 sentences.** The action for this audience is almost always informational: "The first question worth exploring is which category your case falls into, and what the corresponding numbers look like."

### Handling Cooperation Pressure
Never frame cooperation as a simple yes/no. These readers are analytical, give them the framework.

**The 4 variables:**
1. Strength of the government's existing evidence against you (how much do they already have?)
2. Whether you have information the government actually needs (do you have something they want?)
3. Sentencing exposure without cooperation (what's the downside of not cooperating?)
4. Whether other defendants are already cooperating (is there a first-mover dynamic?)

**Proffer risks to name explicitly:** impeachment use, derivative use, the "I didn't lie but my memory was imperfect" trap, the government's ability to withdraw the agreement if they determine you were not fully truthful.

**Never say:** "just cooperate," "never cooperate," "cooperation means betraying people," or "stand strong." Frame it as a strategic calculation with identifiable inputs.

### Handling "My Career Is Over" (Seligman Counter)
Counter catastrophic thinking with specifics, not platitudes.

**Template:**
> Federal cases typically span 1-3 years from indictment to resolution (Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts). During that window, many professionals continue working. Professional licensing consequences vary by state, by licensing board, by the nature of the offense, and by the final disposition. A charge is not a conviction. A conviction on one count is not a conviction on all counts. A negotiated disposition is not a trial loss. Each of these distinctions matters to licensing boards.

**Pair with action:** "Exploring your specific licensing board's standards for the charges you're facing, not 'federal charges' in the abstract, but the specific charge types, is a concrete first step."

### CCO Ordering for Federal Bad News
For sections delivering bad news, high loss amounts, multiple counts, conspiracy charges, RICO allegations:

1. **Compassion:** "Reading an indictment with 23 counts and a loss amount in the millions is disorienting. The numbers look like they were designed to overwhelm. That feeling is universal among defendants in this situation."
2. **Conviction:** "Each count is a separate allegation that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. The loss amount is calculated using methods under the current federal guidelines that often produce figures far higher than any actual financial harm. Neither the count total nor the loss amount is the sentence, they are inputs into a calculation that has multiple stages."
3. **Optimism:** "The gap between the indictment and the final outcome in federal cases is often significant. Understanding how counts are structured, how loss is calculated, and where the calculation can be challenged is the starting point for seeing that gap clearly."

- [ ] No "navigate" in legal context
- [ ] No 3+ sentences starting with the same word in sequence
- [ ] No identical-length list items (vary by 30%+ in word count)
- [ ] No section opening with a definition instead of a scenario
- [ ] No closing paragraph restating the opening (conclusion mirroring)
- [ ] Total body content under 1,800 words
- [ ] No more than 4 H2 sections
- [ ] Each H2 section under 400 words
- [ ] No scene-setting paragraphs (fluorescent lights, sweaty palms)
- [ ] No repeated structural transitions ("So the real question becomes", "But here's what nobody mentions")
- [ ] Product bridge connects free value → paid depth (not a bolted-on CTA)
- [ ] No hedging stacks ("could potentially perhaps be considered")
- [ ] No sycophancy ("great question," "absolutely")
- [ ] No orphan threats (scary stat without action within 2 sentences)
- [ ] Every stat has a named source in parentheses
- [ ] Every legal term defined on first use
- [ ] "Do this right now" section present before first H2
- [ ] Final sentence is agency, not fear or generic CTA
- [ ] No "white collar crime" as casual category, name the specific charge
- [ ] No "criminal" as a noun, use "defendant" or "the person facing charges"
- [ ] No simplified cooperation framing ("just cooperate" or "never talk to agents")
- [ ] Every sentencing guideline reference qualified with "under the current federal guidelines"
- [ ] No loss amount stated without noting it's calculated differently than expected
- [ ] Every conviction rate stat includes the cooperation/trial breakdown context

## 1. Voice Summary

The ImNotAnAttorney Team writes DUI content like a calm friend who knows the system saying "yes, you messed up. One mistake. Here's what the next 90 days actually look like." DUI defendants carry intense shame — they made a mistake and they know it. The voice acknowledges that without rubbing it in, then pivots hard to agency. Time pressure is real and specific: DMV hearing deadlines of 7-15 days in most states create genuine urgency that general defense content doesn't have. The reader is often a first-time offender, a professional, terrified of losing their license and their job. They are scanning this at 2AM on their phone, still tasting the shame of the booking photo and the bail receipt. Every sentence either teaches something specific about the DUI process, restores a sense of control over the next 72 hours, or does both. The emotional register is warmer and more urgent than general defense — shame needs warmth to loosen before information can land, and DMV deadlines don't wait for shame to pass on its own.

**Word budget: 1,000-1,500 words body content. Maximum 1,800. Every sentence must teach, validate, or arm — delete anything that only fills space.**

## 2. Tone Spectrum

| Axis | Position | Why |
|------|----------|-----|
| **Formal <-> Casual** | 25% formal, 75% casual | More casual than general defense because shame needs warmth, not authority. Casual enough to feel like a real person who has been through this. Formal enough that the reader trusts the information about calibration records and DMV procedures. Never slangy. Never stiff. The bar: would someone who already survived a DUI talk like this at a kitchen table? |
| **Clinical <-> Emotional** | 30% clinical, 70% emotional | Lead with emotional validation harder here than in general defense — shame is corrosive and shuts down the reader's ability to absorb information. "You made one mistake" must land before "here's how breathalyzer calibration works." But the clinical details matter enormously — a wrong number about a DMV deadline could cost someone their license. Precision follows validation, never the reverse. |
| **Authoritative <-> Peer** | 40% authoritative, 60% peer | More peer than authority. The reader's trust in authority figures is already damaged — the officer, the system, maybe their own attorney. Authority comes from insider details the reader hasn't encountered yet: rising blood alcohol, mouth alcohol contamination, observation period violations, calibration log gaps. Specificity is credibility. Tone is not. |
| **Urgent <-> Calm** | 60% urgent, 40% calm | More urgent than general defense because DMV deadlines are real and missing them has permanent consequences. "You have days, not weeks" must register without triggering the freeze response that shame already primes. Urgency through specificity — "in most states, you have 7-15 days to request a DMV hearing" — hits harder and calmer than "act now before it's too late." Clock-based, not panic-based. |

## 3. DO/DON'T Examples

### Example 1: Addressing the shame/mistake

- **DO:** "You made a mistake. One mistake on one night. You already know that — you don't need a blog post to tell you. What you need to know is that the mistake is not what determines the next 90 days. The stop, the test, and the timeline — those three things determine your outcome. And all three of them have variables you haven't looked at yet."
- **DON'T:** "Being arrested for DUI can be a traumatic and overwhelming experience. While it is natural to feel shame and regret, it is important to remember that you are not defined by this incident. Many individuals find themselves in similar circumstances."

### Example 2: Explaining DMV hearing deadlines

- **DO:** "Somewhere in the paperwork they handed you that night is a deadline. In most states, you have between 7 and 15 days from the date of your arrest to request a DMV hearing. Miss that window and your license suspension starts automatically — no hearing, no chance to present your side. This is not the criminal case. This is a separate process run by a separate agency with its own rules. Most defendants don't know it exists until the deadline has already passed."
- **DON'T:** "Following a DUI arrest, there are administrative proceedings that must be addressed in addition to the criminal charges. The Department of Motor Vehicles may impose a license suspension pursuant to your state's implied consent statute, and it is critically important to be aware of applicable deadlines for requesting a hearing."

### Example 3: Breathalyzer/field sobriety test challenges

- **DO:** "The breathalyzer is not a blood test. It estimates your BAC (blood alcohol concentration) by measuring alcohol in your breath and running it through a formula. That formula assumes your body is average — average body temperature, average breath volume, average partition ratio. Defendants who request the machine's calibration records sometimes find that the last maintenance was overdue. That doesn't make the number disappear. But it gives a defense attorney something specific to work with. The records are public. You can request them."
- **DON'T:** "Breathalyzer results can sometimes be challenged in court based on potential calibration issues or operator error. An experienced DUI attorney can evaluate the accuracy of the testing equipment and determine whether there are grounds for suppressing the results."

### Example 4: The plea deal pressure

- **DO:** "The prosecutor offers a deal early. Reduced charge. Smaller fine. Probation instead of jail. Sign here, move on with your life. It sounds like relief — and for some defendants, it is the right outcome. But here's the part nobody says out loud: that offer was made before your attorney reviewed the calibration records, before anyone checked whether the officer followed the observation period before the breath test, before the dashcam footage was examined. A plea deal accepted before the evidence is reviewed is a decision made with half the information."
- **DON'T:** "Plea bargaining is a standard component of the criminal justice system, and prosecutors may offer reduced charges or alternative sentencing in exchange for a guilty plea. It is essential to carefully weigh the benefits and drawbacks of any plea offer with your legal counsel before making a decision."

### Example 5: License suspension fears

- **DO:** "Losing your license feels like losing your life. The commute, the school pickup, the grocery run — every piece of daily life runs through that license. Here's what most defendants don't realize: there is a difference between what CAN happen and what TYPICALLY happens. Most states offer restricted or hardship licenses for first-time DUI defendants — limited driving to work, school, and medical appointments. The path from suspension to restricted license has specific steps, and defendants who know those steps before their hearing tend to keep driving sooner."
- **DON'T:** "A DUI conviction can result in the suspension or revocation of your driving privileges for a period ranging from 30 days to several years, depending on your jurisdiction and the specific circumstances of your case. This can have significant impacts on your ability to maintain employment and fulfill daily responsibilities."

### Example 6: Insurance/SR-22 consequences

- **DO:** "After a DUI, your insurance company finds out — either through the conviction or when you need to file an SR-22 (a certificate your insurer sends the state proving you carry the required coverage). Your rates will go up. In most states, the SR-22 requirement lasts 3 years. That's the bad news, and it's real. The part nobody mentions: SR-22 insurance is not a separate policy. It's a filing attached to a standard policy. Shopping between carriers during the SR-22 period can cut the cost significantly. The penalty is fixed. The price you pay for it is not."
- **DON'T:** "Following a DUI conviction, you will likely be required to obtain SR-22 insurance, which is a high-risk insurance filing mandated by your state. This can result in substantially increased premiums for a period of several years and may impact your financial planning."

### Example 7: First-time offender anxiety

- **DO:** "If this is your first offense, your brain is running worst-case scenarios on a loop. Jail. A record. Everyone finding out. Here's what first-offense DUI actually looks like in most states: diversion programs that can lead to reduced or dismissed charges. Restricted licenses that let you keep driving to work. Fines and classes instead of jail time. The maximum penalty printed on your charging document is not the typical outcome — it's the ceiling, and most first-offense cases don't get close to it. That's not wishful thinking. That's sentencing data."
- **DON'T:** "If this is your first DUI offense, you may be eligible for alternative sentencing programs. The penalties for a first-time DUI vary by state but generally include fines, license suspension, and possible jail time. An attorney can help you understand the potential consequences and explore your legal options."

### Example 8: "My life is over" moment

- **DO:** "It feels permanent. The mugshot, the charge, the way your stomach drops every time you think about it. Your brain is telling you three things right now: this is who you are now, this will never go away, and this will touch everything. All three of those are lies your nervous system is telling you under stress. This is one charge from one night. Over 1 million DUI arrests happen in the U.S. every year (FBI Uniform Crime Report). Teachers, nurses, engineers, parents — people who never expected to see the inside of a booking room. The next 90 days are not written yet. Defendants who take one specific action this week — requesting the DMV hearing — are already in a different position than those who freeze."
- **DON'T:** "While it is understandable to feel that a DUI arrest represents a catastrophic event, it is important to maintain perspective. Many individuals who have been arrested for DUI have gone on to resolve their cases favorably and continue with their personal and professional lives."

## Structure Template

Every post follows this skeleton:
1. Hook (2-3 sentences) — name the fear, validate, promise the answer
2. TLDRBox — one-sentence answer + one 5-minute action
3. H2: Core answer (300-400 words) — the thing they came for, with insider detail
4. H2: Actions (200-300 words) — 2-3 things to do RIGHT NOW
5. H2: Common mistake (150-200 words) — one error + how to avoid it
6. Agency close (2 sentences) — competence, not fear
7. Product bridge (3-4 sentences) — free value → paid depth for YOUR case

Total: 1,000-1,500 words. The reader finishes in 3-4 minutes on their phone.
The product bridge earns its place because the free content already delivered value.

| "deep dive" | AI slop | "close look" or "breakdown" |
| "it's important to note" | Filler | Delete — just say the thing |
| "comprehensive guide" | AI self-description | Delete |
| "in today's landscape" | AI slop | Delete |
| "consult your attorney" | Tone-deaf — reader's attorney may not be responsive | "bring this to your attorney" or "worth exploring with your attorney" |
| "verify with your attorney" | Implies verification step that may not happen | Rephrase with specific action |
| "we recommend" / "we advise" | UPL violation | "defendants in this situation often explore..." |

| "drunk" / "intoxicated" (as labels for the reader) | Shame-reinforcing | "over the limit" or "above the legal BAC" or "charged with DUI" |

| "the consequences are severe" (standalone) | Orphan threat without action | Pair with what defendants can do about it |

### Terms to Define Inline (first use only)

- **BAC** — "your BAC (blood alcohol concentration) — the number the breath or blood test measured"
- **FST** — "a field sobriety test (FST) — the walk-and-turn, one-leg stand, and eye-tracking exercises the officer asks you to perform roadside"
- **Implied consent** — "implied consent — the rule that says by driving on public roads, you already agreed to take a chemical test if asked"
- **SR-22** — "an SR-22 — a certificate your insurance company files with the state proving you carry the required coverage after a DUI"
- **Administrative suspension** — "an administrative suspension — a license suspension handled by the DMV, separate from anything the criminal court does"
- **Rising blood alcohol** — "rising blood alcohol — the idea that your BAC was still climbing at the time of the test because your body hadn't finished absorbing the alcohol you consumed"
- **Observation period** — "the observation period — a window (usually 15-20 minutes) where the officer is supposed to continuously watch you before administering the breath test to make sure nothing affects the result"
- **Partition ratio** — "the partition ratio — the mathematical assumption the breathalyzer uses to convert the amount of alcohol in your breath to an estimated blood alcohol level"

## 6. Emotional Calibration

### Handling Shame (Witte Threat-Efficacy — Adapted)

**Rule:** Acknowledge the mistake in 1 sentence maximum, then pivot to variables the defendant CAN control. Never return to the shame. The shame spiral is the single biggest threat to the reader actually doing something useful — if the voice keeps touching the wound, the reader closes the tab.

**Pattern:**
1. One sentence: "You made a mistake. One mistake."
2. Immediate pivot: "Here's what determines the next 90 days — and it isn't the mistake."
3. Variables they control: the DMV hearing request, evidence preservation, attorney questions, breathalyzer challenges.

**Example:**
> You made a bad call on one night. That part is done. What's not done: whether you request the DMV hearing before the deadline, whether you pull the breathalyzer's calibration records, whether you write down what you remember about the stop while the details are still fresh. The arrest is a fixed point. Everything after it is a variable — and variables are where outcomes change.

**What NOT to do:**
> "We know you're feeling terrible right now. Everyone makes mistakes, and this doesn't make you a bad person. Many good people find themselves in this situation, and you shouldn't beat yourself up about it." [Returns to shame three times in four sentences. Reader spirals deeper.]

### Handling "First Offense" Anxiety (Specificity Counter)

**Rule:** Counter catastrophizing with specifics — what first-time DUI actually looks like in most states. The reader's brain is projecting the maximum penalty onto their situation. The antidote is real data, not reassurance.

**Pattern:**
1. Name what they're imagining: "Your brain is running the worst-case version."
2. Counter with the typical case: diversion programs, restricted licenses, fines and classes instead of jail.
3. Bridge to action: "Here's what first-time defendants who prepare tend to see."

**Example:**
> Most states have diversion programs specifically designed for first-time DUI defendants. Complete the requirements — usually alcohol education classes, community service, and a monitoring period — and the charge can be reduced or dismissed. Restricted licenses allow driving to work, school, and medical appointments during the suspension period. The maximum penalty on your charging document is the ceiling, not the floor. Sentencing data shows that most first-offense cases with BAC under .15 (NHTSA threshold for aggravated DUI in most states) resolve without jail time.

### CCO Ordering for DUI Bad News (High BAC, Refusal, Accident)

When delivering particularly heavy DUI-specific bad news — a high BAC result, a test refusal with enhanced penalties, or a DUI involving an accident — follow this sequence:

1. **Compassion:** Name what the reader is feeling. One sentence. "A BAC of .15 or higher changes the math. That number probably hit you like a wall."
2. **Conviction:** State the reality clearly. No softening. "Most states treat a BAC above .15 as an aggravated DUI, which carries enhanced penalties — higher fines, longer license suspension, mandatory ignition interlock."
3. **Optimism:** End with an action or a reason to keep going. "Enhanced penalties also mean enhanced scrutiny of the testing procedure. Every step of the breath test protocol — the observation period, the calibration records, the officer's training certification — matters more at higher BAC levels, not less. Defendants who request these records are looking at the same evidence a defense attorney needs."

**Example (refusal):**
> Refusing the breath test probably felt like protecting yourself. In most states, the refusal itself triggers an automatic license suspension — often longer than the suspension for a failed test. That's the implied consent law at work. But refusal cases also mean the prosecution has no chemical test result. The case then rests on the officer's observations, the field sobriety tests, and the dashcam footage. Every one of those has documented weaknesses that defense attorneys examine. A refusal changes the terrain. It doesn't end the fight.

## 7. Anti-Slop Checklist

Before publishing any DUI blog post, check every item. Any match triggers a rewrite of that section.

### Structural Slop (inherited from general-defense)
- [ ] No "it's important to note/understand/remember" (filler — just say it)
- [ ] No "navigate" in legal context
- [ ] No 3+ sentences starting with the same word in sequence
- [ ] No identical-length list items (vary by 30%+ in word count)
- [ ] No section opening with a definition instead of a scenario
- [ ] No closing paragraph restating the opening (conclusion mirroring)
- [ ] Total body content under 1,800 words
- [ ] No more than 4 H2 sections
- [ ] Each H2 section under 400 words
- [ ] No scene-setting paragraphs (fluorescent lights, sweaty palms)
- [ ] No repeated structural transitions ("So the real question becomes", "But here's what nobody mentions")
- [ ] Product bridge connects free value → paid depth (not a bolted-on CTA)
- [ ] No hedging stacks ("could potentially perhaps be considered")
- [ ] No sycophancy ("great question," "absolutely")
- [ ] No orphan threats (scary stat without action within 2 sentences)
- [ ] Every stat has a named source in parentheses
- [ ] Every legal term defined on first use
- [ ] "Do this right now" section present before first H2
- [ ] Final sentence is agency, not fear or generic CTA

### DUI-Specific Slop
- [ ] No "drunk driving" — use "DUI" or "impaired driving"
- [ ] No "failed the test" — tests can be challenged; use "the test showed" or "the test indicated"
- [ ] No minimizing the mistake ("it happens to everyone," "don't beat yourself up") — acknowledge once, move on, never return
- [ ] Every DMV deadline qualified with "in most states" or a specific state named — never state a bare number as universal
- [ ] No scare tactics about license loss without the reinstatement path in the same section — every suspension mention paired with restricted license or reinstatement steps
- [ ] No "drunk" or "intoxicated" as labels for the reader — use "charged with DUI" or "above the legal BAC"
- [ ] No blame language ("you should have planned ahead," "next time call an Uber")
- [ ] No moralizing ("drinking and driving is dangerous," "you put lives at risk")
- [ ] No assuming guilt — the reader is charged, not convicted; use "after the arrest" not "when you were driving drunk"
- [ ] No conflating DMV and criminal tracks — always clarify they are separate processes with separate deadlines
- [ ] No "worst case scenario" framing without typical-case data in the same paragraph
- [ ] No "blow" — use "provide a breath sample"

### Legal Safety Slop (inherited from general-defense)
- [ ] Any stat, statute, or procedural claim traceable to a verified source with URL
- [ ] No "verify with your attorney" or "consult a legal professional"
- [ ] No "you should argue" or "we advise" phrasing
- [ ] No case law citations in pre-purchase content
- [ ] No state-specific penalty stated as universal
- [ ] No sentence directing the reader to take a specific legal action — reframe as what defendants in similar situations have done or what the statute allows

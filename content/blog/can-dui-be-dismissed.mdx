<TLDRBox>
Yes, DUI cases are dismissed when police made errors during the traffic stop, field sobriety tests, or chemical testing. Common grounds: illegal stop, breathalyzer calibration failure, NHTSA protocol violations, missing dashcam footage.
</TLDRBox>

You blew over the limit. Or you think you did. Or you refused the test entirely. And now your attorney wants to talk about plea deals.

But here's what most DUI defendants don't know: **DUI cases get dismissed all the time.** Not because of legal technicalities that only TV lawyers know, but because of real, specific errors that police officers make during traffic stops, field sobriety tests, and chemical testing.

Your attorney should be investigating every single one of these. If they're not, you're leaving defenses on the table.

## Can a DUI Charge Be Dismissed?

You're staring at the charge sheet on your kitchen counter. The words "driving under the influence" look permanent, final, like the outcome has already been decided. It hasn't.

Yes, a DUI can be dismissed. The most common grounds for dismissal are:

1. **Illegal traffic stop**, the officer lacked reasonable suspicion
2. **Field sobriety test errors**, NHTSA protocol violations during administration
3. **Breathalyzer failures**, calibration, operator certification, or observation period issues
4. **Blood test errors**, contamination, improper storage, or chain of custody breaks
5. **No probable cause for arrest**, insufficient evidence of impairment beyond alcohol odor
6. **Miranda violations**, questioning without proper warnings after arrest
7. **Rising blood alcohol**, BAC was below the limit while actually driving

But here's what nobody mentions: most of these defenses require your attorney to actively investigate. They don't surface on their own. If your attorney hasn't requested calibration records, watched the dashcam, or compared the police report to the footage, these defenses might exist in your case and nobody would ever know.

**A DUI defense that nobody investigates is a defense that never exists.**

Each defense is explained in detail below. Read each one and ask yourself: has my attorney checked for this?

## Defense 1: The Traffic Stop Was Illegal

You were driving home. The road was empty. Then blue lights appeared behind you. You pulled over, and sixty minutes later you were in handcuffs. But the question nobody asked that night is: why did the officer pull you over in the first place?

The Fourth Amendment requires police to have **reasonable suspicion** to stop your vehicle. That means the officer needed a specific, articulable reason to pull you over.

**Valid reasons for a stop:**
- Traffic violations (speeding, running a red light, failure to signal)
- Equipment violations (broken taillight, expired registration)
- Driving behavior consistent with impairment (weaving across lanes, extreme speed variation)

**Questionable reasons that get challenged:**
- "Weaving within your lane", courts are split on this, and many find it insufficient
- "Driving slowly", going 5 mph under the speed limit is not reasonable suspicion
- Anonymous tips without corroboration
- Stopping you for a minor equipment violation as a pretext to investigate DUI

But here's what nobody mentions: the officer's stated reason for the stop is in the police report. The dashcam footage shows what actually happened. When those two things don't match, everything that followed, the field sobriety tests, the breathalyzer, the arrest, becomes suppressible.

**If the dashcam doesn't match the police report, the entire case can unravel from the first frame.**

**What your attorney should be doing:** Reviewing the dashcam footage and the officer's report for the stated reason for the stop. If the reason was insufficient, everything that followed, field sobriety tests, breathalyzer, arrest, can be suppressed.

**Questions to ask:** "Was the traffic stop legal? What was the officer's stated reason? Does the dashcam support that reason?"

Read your police report tonight and find the officer's stated reason for the stop. Write it down. That's the first thing to discuss at your next attorney meeting.

## Defense 2: Field Sobriety Tests Were Administered Incorrectly

You're standing on the shoulder of a highway. Eighteen-wheelers are blowing past at 65 mph. The pavement is gravel. The officer says, "Walk nine steps heel-to-toe, turn, and walk nine steps back." This is supposed to be a scientific test.

The Standardized Field Sobriety Tests (SFSTs), Horizontal Gaze Nystagmus (HGN), Walk-and-Turn, and One-Leg Stand, have strict protocols from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), the federal agency that sets the rules police must follow during sobriety testing.

**Common errors officers make:**
- Administering tests on uneven or sloped surfaces
- Failing to demonstrate the tests properly
- Not asking about medical conditions that affect balance (inner ear problems, leg injuries, neurological conditions)
- Administering tests in poor lighting or adverse weather
- Using non-standardized tests (finger-to-nose, alphabet recitation) that have no scientific validation
- Misscoring the tests

But here's what nobody mentions: NHTSA's own research shows these tests are wrong up to a third of the time even under perfect laboratory conditions. Your test was not administered under perfect conditions. It was administered at midnight, on the side of a road, with flashing lights and traffic, by an officer who may have rushed the protocol.

**NHTSA's own data says field sobriety tests produce false positives up to a third of the time, and that's under perfect conditions yours were not.**

**What your attorney should be doing:** Obtaining the officer's SFST certification records, reviewing body cam footage of the test administration, and comparing against NHTSA protocols step by step. Our post on [NHTSA field sobriety test standards](/blog/field-sobriety-test-standards) breaks down every deviation that matters.

**Questions to ask:** "Have you reviewed the field sobriety test administration against NHTSA standards? Were there any deviations?"

Ask your attorney whether the officer's SFST certification is current and whether the bodycam captured the full test administration. Those two facts determine whether the test results are admissible.

## Defense 3: Breathalyzer Results Are Unreliable

The officer tells you to blow. You blow. A number appears on the screen: .09. That number feels like a prison sentence. But that number came from a machine, and machines need maintenance, calibration, and certified operators to produce reliable results.

Breathalyzer machines are not infallible. They require regular calibration, proper maintenance, and certified operators.

**Common breathalyzer issues:**
- **Calibration:** The machine wasn't calibrated within the required schedule
- **Operator certification:** The person administering the test wasn't properly trained or certified
- **Observation period:** Most states require a 15-20 minute observation period before testing (to ensure you didn't burp, vomit, or put anything in your mouth). If this was skipped or shortened, results are challengeable
- **Radio frequency interference:** Certain electronic devices can affect breathalyzer readings
- **Mouth alcohol:** Recent use of mouthwash, breath spray, or even acid reflux can inflate readings
- **Temperature:** Body temperature affects breath alcohol readings, a fever can produce falsely high results
- **Margin of error:** Breathalyzers have an inherent margin of error (typically plus or minus .005-.02 per Forensic Science International standards). If you blew a .08 or .09, the margin of error alone could put you under the limit

So the real question becomes: was the machine working correctly on the night it tested you? The only way to answer that is through the calibration records, and your attorney has to request them.

**If you blew .08 or .09, the machine's own margin of error could mean you were under the legal limit.**

**What your attorney should be doing:** Subpoenaing the breathalyzer's maintenance and [calibration records](/blog/breathalyzer-calibration-records), the operator's certification, and the observation period log. If any of these are deficient, the BAC result can be challenged.

**Questions to ask:** "Have you requested the breathalyzer calibration records? When was the machine last calibrated? Is the operator certified?"

Ask your attorney these three questions at your next meeting. Write down the answers. If they haven't requested calibration records yet, ask when they will.

## Defense 4: Blood Test Errors

The phlebotomist snaps on latex gloves. You look away as the needle goes in. The vial fills. What you don't see: where that vial goes next, how it's stored, who handles it, and whether anyone breaks the chain of custody along the way.

If your BAC was measured via blood draw, different challenges apply:

- **Chain of custody:** Was the blood sample properly handled from draw to lab?
- **Fermentation:** Improperly stored blood samples can ferment, producing alcohol that wasn't there at the time of the draw
- **Contamination:** Was the draw site swabbed with a non-alcohol antiseptic? (Using alcohol swabs can contaminate the sample)
- **Timing:** Blood alcohol levels rise after drinking. If you were tested well after driving, your BAC at the time of testing may have been higher than your BAC while driving (this is called "rising blood alcohol defense")
- **Lab accreditation:** Is the lab accredited? Has the analyst had results questioned before?

But here's what nobody mentions: blood test chain of custody documentation is often incomplete. A missing signature, an unaccounted-for hour between the draw and the lab, improper storage temperature, any of these gaps means the prosecution cannot prove the sample that was tested is the same sample that was drawn from you.

**A blood test without an unbroken chain of custody is a number without a source, and numbers without sources are not evidence.**

**What your attorney should be doing:** Requesting the complete blood testing documentation, chain of custody forms, and lab records.

**Questions to ask:** "Have you reviewed the blood test chain of custody? Was the blood properly stored? What was the time between the stop and the blood draw?"

Check your arrest paperwork for the time of the stop and the time of the blood draw. Calculate the gap. If it's more than 30 minutes, the rising blood alcohol defense may apply. Bring those two timestamps to your attorney.

## Defense 5: No Probable Cause for Arrest

The officer says, "I'm placing you under arrest for DUI." You weren't weaving. You passed the walk-and-turn. You were polite and coherent. The only thing the officer noted was "the odor of alcohol." One observation. That's the entire basis for your arrest.

Even if the stop was legal, the officer needs **probable cause** to arrest you for DUI. Probable cause requires more than just the smell of alcohol or a single failed field sobriety test.

**Challenge points:**
- Did the officer have sufficient evidence of impairment beyond alcohol odor?
- Were there alternative explanations for observed behavior (fatigue, medication, medical conditions)?
- Did the officer's observations match the dashcam/bodycam footage?

So the real question becomes: does the totality of the evidence actually support an arrest, or does it only support the fact that you had a drink? Having a drink is legal. Being impaired is not. The prosecution has to prove the second one.

**The smell of alcohol is not probable cause for arrest, it is evidence that someone had a drink, which is legal.**

Watch your dashcam or bodycam footage (if available through discovery) and note how you appeared. Were you steady? Coherent? Cooperative? Write down your observations and compare them to what the officer wrote in the report.

## Defense 6: Miranda and Right to Counsel Violations

The back seat of the patrol car is hard plastic. The door is locked from the outside. The officer turns around and asks, "So how many drinks did you have tonight?" No Miranda warning. No rights read. Just a question designed to build the case against you.

If you were questioned about drinking after being arrested without Miranda warnings, your statements may be suppressed. Additionally, in some states you have the right to consult with an attorney before deciding whether to take a chemical test.

But here's what nobody mentions: the line between "pre-arrest investigation" (Miranda not required) and "post-arrest interrogation" (Miranda required) is where this defense lives. If the officer asked you questions after you were effectively in custody, handcuffed, in the back of the car, not free to leave, and Miranda wasn't read, those statements are challengeable.

**Statements made after arrest without Miranda warnings can be suppressed, and without your statements, the prosecution's case gets thinner.**

Review your arrest paperwork for when Miranda was read. Compare that timestamp to when you were placed in handcuffs. If questions were asked in between, note them for your attorney.

## Defense 7: Rising Blood Alcohol

You left the restaurant 20 minutes ago. You had your last glass of wine at 9:40 PM. You were pulled over at 10:05 PM. Your blood was drawn at 10:50 PM, a full 70 minutes after your last drink. In that time, your body was still absorbing alcohol.

Alcohol takes 30-90 minutes to fully absorb (National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism). If you had your last drink shortly before driving, your BAC may have been below .08 while you were actually driving, and only rose above .08 by the time you were tested.

This is a legitimate defense that requires expert testimony, but it's powerful when the facts support it.

So the real question becomes: what was your BAC at the time of driving, not at the time of testing? The prosecution tests you 30, 45, 60 minutes after the stop. Your BAC at the time of the test is not necessarily your BAC at the time of driving.

**The prosecution measures your BAC at the time of the test, not at the time of driving. Those can be two very different numbers.**

Write down the time of your last drink, the time of the traffic stop, and the time of the chemical test. Those three timestamps are the foundation of the rising blood alcohol defense. Bring them to your attorney.

## What Your Attorney Should Be Doing Right Now

You've read seven defenses. You understand the evidence challenges. Now you're sitting with a single uncomfortable question: has my attorney checked any of this?

If you've been charged with DUI and your attorney hasn't discussed at least some of these defenses with you, ask why.

A competent DUI attorney should:
1. Review dashcam and bodycam footage
2. Analyze the stated reason for the traffic stop
3. Evaluate SFST administration against NHTSA standards
4. Subpoena breathalyzer calibration and maintenance records
5. Review blood test chain of custody (if applicable)
6. Check officer certification and training records
7. Investigate the observation period compliance
8. Consider rising blood alcohol defense
9. File suppression motions for any violations found

But here's what nobody mentions: this checklist is not optional, it is the minimum standard of competent DUI defense. An attorney who skips these steps and goes straight to plea negotiations is not saving you time. They are costing you defenses.

**This nine-point checklist is not above and beyond, it is the minimum standard of competent DUI defense.**

If your attorney's only strategy is "let's see what they offer", they're not doing their job.

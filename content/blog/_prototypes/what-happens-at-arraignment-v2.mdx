<TLDRBox>
Arraignment is a 5-15 minute hearing where you hear the formal charges, enter a plea (almost always not guilty), and the judge sets bail conditions and your next court date. You will not be sentenced at arraignment.

**Your Next Step:** Set aside your court outfit tonight and put your case number, attorney's name, and a pen in a bag by the door. That takes 5 minutes and eliminates half the morning-of stress.
</TLDRBox>

You have a court date and you've never been in a courtroom before. You don't know where to stand, what to say, or what happens if you say the wrong thing.

Here's the reality: arraignment is the least dramatic event in your entire case. But walking in prepared makes you look completely different to the judge than stumbling in confused. Preparation is the one thing you control.

## What to Do Before You Walk In

Dress like a job interview — khakis and a button-down, or a blouse and slacks. No hats, no sunglasses, no graphic tees. Judges notice.

Bring only these:
- Your attorney's name and phone number
- Photo ID
- Your case number (on your bail paperwork or summons)
- A pen and paper

Arrive 30 minutes early. Find parking, clear security, find the right courtroom. Being late to court is one of the worst first impressions you can make.

**Leave your phone on silent. Judges have held people in contempt for a ringing phone.**

## What Actually Happens (5-15 Minutes)

You check in with the clerk, then wait. The wait can be hours — courts batch dozens of cases into one session. Use that time: watch the cases before yours. By the fifth one, you'll know the entire routine.

When your name is called, here's the sequence:

1. The judge reads the formal charges. Listen — make sure they match what you were arrested for. If something sounds wrong, write it down for your attorney.
2. You enter a plea. Your attorney will say **not guilty**. This is standard. It does not mean you're lying — it means you're preserving your rights while your attorney reviews the evidence.
3. The judge sets or confirms bail conditions — could be monetary bail, personal recognizance (released on your promise to return), travel restrictions, or no-contact orders.
4. You get your next court date. Write it down.

That's it. You walk out.

**Never plead guilty at arraignment. Even if you think the evidence is overwhelming. There may be defenses, procedural issues, or negotiating leverage you don't know about yet.**

## The 48 Hours After Matter More Than the Hearing

Most defendants feel relieved after arraignment, go home, and wait. Waiting is how cases fall through the cracks.

Within a few days, send your attorney these five questions. Copy-paste them right now into a draft:

1. **"When will you receive discovery?"** — The prosecution's evidence should be coming soon.
2. **"What's our initial strategy?"** — Even preliminary thoughts help you understand direction.
3. **"Are there any immediate deadlines?"** — Some motions have early windows that close fast.
4. **"When should I expect to hear from you next?"** — Set this expectation now, not after months of silence.
5. **"Is there anything I should be doing?"** — Character letters, treatment programs, employment docs.

**The real defense starts the day after arraignment, not the day of.**

## The One Rule for Every Courthouse Visit

After the hearing, a police officer might make small talk in the hallway. A prosecutor might seem friendly. A co-defendant might want to compare notes.

Say nothing. To anyone. About anything related to your case.

Practice this sentence until it's automatic: **"I'd like to speak with my attorney."**

More defendants hurt their cases in courthouse hallways than in courtrooms.

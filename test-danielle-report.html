Understanding Your Charges

In Texas, what's commonly called "DUI" is charged as DWI — Driving While Intoxicated — under Texas Penal Code § 49.04. Here's what the prosecution must prove at trial:

Element Prosecution Must ProvePlain EnglishQuestion for Your Attorney
Operation of a motor vehicleYou were driving or in physical control of a vehicle"Is there any question about whether I was actually driving when contact was made?"
In a public placeThe driving happened on a road, highway, or place open to the public"Does the location of the checkpoint raise any issues?"
While intoxicatedEither (a) BAC of 0.08 or higher OR (b) loss of normal use of mental or physical faculties due to alcohol"Given my BAC was .09, what are the options for challenging whether I was actually intoxicated at the time I was driving?"
Penalty Range — First Offense DWI (Class B Misdemeanor):
  • 72 hours to 180 days in county jail (Texas Penal Code § 49.04(b))
  • Fine up to $2,000
  • License suspension of 90 days to 1 year (Texas Transportation Code § 521.344)
  • Possible community supervision (probation) of up to 2 years

    • These are statutory maximums, not predictions. For a first offense with a .09 BAC and no accident, the realistic outcome often falls well below these ceilings. The questions in this report help you understand where YOUR case falls.

    Your Rights in This Process:
  • Right to see all evidence against you — Texas Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 39.14 (the "Michael Morton Act") requires the prosecution to disclose its evidence.
  • Right to be consulted before any plea is entered on your behalf.
  • Right to understand the strategy your attorney is pursuing.
  • Right to a second legal opinion at any time.
    • Important — Your Nursing License: You told us you're a nurse. A DWI conviction — or even certain plea outcomes — may need to be reported to the BON. This makes the type of resolution critical, not just whether you're convicted. Q12 addresses this directly.

    How to Document Everything

  • During the meeting: Bring a pen and paper. Write down your attorney's answers to each question — even shorthand notes help.

    Q1 ★ Golden Question — If You Only Ask One Question, Ask This One

    "My BAC came back at .09 and the legal limit is .08. What's the margin of error on the breathalyzer they used, and is there a way to challenge that reading?" Why it matters: You told us your BAC was .09 — just .01 above the legal limit. Justin McShane, a forensic lawyer-scientist and American Chemical Society member, has documented that breath testing instruments carry an inherent margin of error, typically ±0.005 to ±0.02 depending on the device, calibration, and conditions. At .09, your reading may fall within that margin. Your attorney should be evaluating the specific instrument's maintenance records, calibration logs, and the operator's certification. Good answer: "I've requested the calibration and maintenance records for the specific device used. The margin of error on that model is [X], and here's what that means for our case." If the answer is vague: "Can you help me understand — has the breathalyzer's calibration history been requested yet, and when will we know if there's a basis to challenge the reading?" What to listen for: If your attorney hasn't requested calibration records yet, that's something to note — not necessarily a problem, but an action item. Ask: "What's the timeline for getting those records?" Write down their answer. If no timeline is given after a follow-up, move to Step 4 in Your Advocacy Steps.

    Q2 — The Stop Itself

    "Walk me through the legal basis for why I was stopped. You mentioned it was a checkpoint — are DWI checkpoints legal in Texas?" Why it matters: You told us you were stopped at a DUI checkpoint. This is significant: Texas law enforcement generally does not conduct sobriety checkpoints because Texas courts have interpreted the state constitution to provide stronger protection against warrantless stops than federal law requires. While the U.S. Supreme Court upheld checkpoints in Michigan v. Sitz (1990), the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals has signaled skepticism. Lawrence Taylor's Drunk Driving Defense methodology begins with challenging the legality of every stop — and in Texas, a checkpoint stop may be challengeable on state constitutional grounds. Good answer: "It was actually a [license check / 'no refusal' initiative / individual traffic stop], and here's the legal basis for the stop..." If the answer is vague: "If the stop itself wasn't legally justified, what would that mean for the breathalyzer results and everything that came after?" What to listen for: If your attorney hasn't researched the nature of the stop, note it and say: "This seems important — can we make sure to look into it before March 20?" Document their response. If no progress after follow-up, move to Step 4.

    Q6 — Rising BAC Defense

    "I had two glasses of wine over about three hours before I drove. Is it possible my BAC was actually below .08 when I was driving and only reached .09 by the time I was tested?" Why it matters: You told us you had two glasses of wine over three hours. Justin McShane's forensic methodology highlights that BAC rises for 30–90 minutes after the last drink and can peak well after someone has stopped drinking. If time passed between when you were driving and when you were tested, your BAC at the time of driving may have been lower than .08. This is called the "rising BAC defense," and at .09, it's directly relevant. Good answer: "I've looked at the timeline between the stop and the breath test. Here's the gap and what it means for a rising BAC argument..." If the answer is vague: "Can we figure out exactly how much time passed between when I was stopped and when the breath test was given?" What to listen for: The time gap between the stop and the test is a factual question with a specific answer. If your attorney can't give it yet, ask when they'll have it.

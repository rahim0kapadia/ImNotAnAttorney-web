### 3b. Defense Theories

These are established categories of defense inquiry for consent-search drug cases. Your attorney determines which apply to your specific facts.

**Consent Validity Challenge (Lichtman methodology)**
Jeffrey Lichtman's approach to evidence suppression begins with the consent interaction itself. Key questions: Did the officer clearly inform you of your right to refuse? Were you free to leave? Did the officer's tone, positioning, or number of officers present create an atmosphere of coercion? Was consent written or verbal? Body camera footage is the decisive evidence.

**Search Scope Challenge**
Even if consent was valid, the search must stay within the scope consented to. If you consented to a vehicle search but the officer searched your person, or if you consented to look in the trunk but the officer opened sealed containers, the evidence found outside that scope may be suppressible.

**Chain of Custody Challenge (Chapman methodology)**
Ron Chapman II's approach examines whether the substance was properly handled from seizure to lab. Gaps in chain of custody, who had the evidence, where it was stored, whether it was properly sealed, can undermine the prosecution's ability to prove the substance is what they claim.

**Lab Analysis Challenge**
The prosecution must prove the substance is cannabis through certified lab testing. Field tests are presumptive, not confirmatory. If the lab analysis has procedural issues or the testing methodology is questioned, the identification itself becomes contested.

- **If "I've requested it but haven't reviewed it yet" →** Ask when she expects to review it and whether it will happen before the hearing.
- **If "I haven't requested it" →** "Can we request it now? Retention policies vary, footage can be deleted." This is time-sensitive.

Whether footage has been obtained AND reviewed, not just requested, tells you where preparation stands.

**Q4:** "Was my consent to search truly voluntary? Did the officer tell me I could refuse? Does the body camera show anything that suggests coercion?"

Ron Chapman II's forensic approach to drug evidence starts with how the evidence was obtained. Voluntariness factors include: Were you told you could refuse? Were multiple officers present? Was the officer's tone commanding or requesting? Were you free to leave? Did you feel you had a choice? Your memory of the interaction plus the footage tells the full story. If she hasn't analyzed this, that's a discovery priority.

---

**Q5:** "Am I eligible for pre-trial intervention in Duval County? Have you applied, and is there a deadline?"

The program means dismissal, the best possible outcome for your record. Under Florida's diversion statutes, first-time misdemeanor drug offenders are generally eligible. The question is whether Whitfield has pursued this path. Listen for whether she's applied or investigated eligibility vs. "I'll look into it." If she hasn't: "Could you check this week? I want to make sure we don't miss an application window."

**Q6:** "Have you received all discovery materials, police report, body camera footage, lab analysis, chain of custody documentation, and officer training records?"

| She lists specific items received and outstanding | "I think so" or "I'll have to check" |
| Gives a timeline for outstanding items | Can't name what's been received |
| Has already reviewed what she has | Hasn't opened the file recently |

### Motions & Deadlines

**Q7 + Q8** (ask together, they're related):

"Are there any motions, suppression, discovery compulsion, that we should consider filing before May 8?" AND "Is there a deadline for diversion applications in Duval County?"

Both are deadline-sensitive. Pre-trial motions are generally filed before the hearing, and diversion applications may have their own timeline. If she answers Q7 with "I haven't looked into it," ask Q8 immediately, it tests whether time-sensitive items are being tracked. A clear yes or no on eligibility is factual. If vague: "Can you check this week? The hearing is approaching."

### Plea Evaluation

**Q9:** "If a plea is offered, is it the standard first offer for misdemeanor cannabis, or has it been negotiated based on my situation?"

- **If yes, it's standard →** "What would a negotiated offer look like, and what leverage do we have?"
- **If it's been negotiated →** "What did the original offer look like, and what moved the needle?"
- **If no plea offered yet →** "When do plea discussions typically begin in Duval County misdemeanor cases?"

**Q10:** "What are the realistic total costs of each path, diversion program fees vs. conviction fines, court costs, and the license suspension impact?"

> A financial comparison helps you evaluate options with data. The program has fees but avoids conviction consequences. Conviction carries fines, court costs, insurance impacts from the license suspension (§ 322.055), and the long-term cost of a criminal record on employment and housing applications.

### Judge & Jurisdiction

**Q11:** "What is Judge Clarkson's approach to first-offense cannabis cases? Does he favor diversion?"

Judge tendencies shape strategy directly. If she hasn't appeared before him, asking her to consult a colleague who has is reasonable and standard.

### Consequences

**Q12 + Q13** (pair these, the second follows naturally from the first):

First ask: "What are the realistic best-case and worst-case outcomes, given everything you know right now?"
Then: "If I don't accept a plea, what happens? Does the offer typically get worse, stay the same, or is there room?"

A calibrated range helps you decide with data instead of fear or hope. The second question tests whether any current offer is a ceiling or a floor. Follow-up: "Have you seen cases where the offer improved after a suppression motion was filed?"

**Q14:** "What exactly happened during the consent interaction, from your review of the evidence, was there anything that suggests my consent wasn't freely given?"

| What to listen for | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| "The footage shows the officer informed you of your right to refuse" | Consent likely valid, shifts focus to other defense angles |
| "The officer's tone was commanding and you weren't told you could refuse" | Strong basis for suppression motion |
| "I haven't reviewed the footage yet" | Your #1 defense angle is unexplored after 4 months, escalate |

**Q15:** "Is there anything about my case that concerns you or that I should know?"

This open-ended question invites information attorneys might not volunteer. Candor, even if it includes concerns, is what enables informed decisions.

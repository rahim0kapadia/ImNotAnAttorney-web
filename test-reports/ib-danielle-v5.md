### 6i. When the Conversation Gets Difficult

**Scenario 1: "Trust me, I'm handling it."**
- **What's happening:** This may be genuine, or it may be a pattern of deflecting questions. Attorneys who are actively working cases can usually name what they're doing.
- **What you can say:** "I do trust you, I'd just like to understand what's being handled so I can be a better partner in this. Can you walk me through what you've done on my case since our last meeting?"
- **Why it works:** Positions you as collaborative, not combative. A prepared attorney will have specifics.

**Scenario 2: "You don't need to worry about that."**
- **What's happening:** This can mean the issue genuinely isn't relevant, or that the attorney doesn't want to spend time explaining. For near-threshold cases, the details matter.
- **What you can say:** "I'm not trying to worry, I'm trying to understand. Can you help me see why that part isn't relevant to my case?"
- **Why it works:** Validates their expertise while holding space for your question.

**Scenario 3: The attorney seems rushed.**
- **What's happening:** Public defenders often have multiple hearings the same day. Time pressure is real.
- **What you can say:** "I know you're busy today. Can we schedule a separate 15-minute call this week to go through my questions? I have them written down."
- **Why it works:** Acknowledges their reality. A written agenda respects their time.

**Scenario 4: Won't answer a specific question.**
- **What's happening:** Some attorneys avoid answering questions they don't yet know the answer to. Others avoid questions that reveal work they haven't done.
- **What you can say:** "I understand if you need time to look into it. Can I expect an answer by [specific date]? I'll follow up in writing."
- **Why it works:** Creates a concrete timeline and a paper trail, gently.

### 6j. Your Advocacy Steps

These steps are collaborative, designed to get you and Torres working together effectively:

**Step 1:** Send the email (Section 6b). Done today.
**Step 2:** Follow up by phone if no response within 3-5 days. Reference your email and request a specific time.
**Step 3:** Send the follow-up email (Section 6e) after 5-7 days. This creates a second written record with timestamped questions.

**A note about second opinions and the "blue wall":** If you do seek a second opinion, be aware that many attorneys are reluctant to criticize another attorney's work directly, it's professional courtesy, not conspiracy. Focus your second opinion on "what would you do differently?" rather than "is my attorney bad?" The answer to the first question is more useful.

## Appendix D: Targeted Follow-Up Questions

These questions go deeper than the five on your Meeting Ready Sheet. Bring any that resonate to your next conversation with Torres.

### Case Strategy

**Q1, The Golden Question (if you only ask one, ask this one):**
"You said my BAC of 0.09 is borderline. Have you identified the specific instrument used, pulled its calibration history, and determined whether the margin of error creates a viable defense, or are you still working on that?"

**Why it matters:** Justin McShane's framework for challenging near-threshold readings starts with one question: is this instrument reliable enough to distinguish 0.08 from 0.09? The answer lives in the calibration records, not the reading itself.

**A strong response:** Torres can name the instrument model, has reviewed or requested calibration records, and can articulate whether precision is a viable defense theory.

**If he seems unsure:** "When do you expect to have the calibration records? I want to understand the timeline for this part of the defense."

**The key signal:** Whether Torres is building a case around the reading's reliability or treating the 0.09 as a settled fact. Those are two different defense postures.

---

**Q2:** "Have you personally reviewed the body camera footage from my stop, and if so, did the officer follow SFST administration protocols correctly?"

**Background:** William "Bubba" Head's methodology centers on identifying administration errors in field sobriety tests. NHTSA protocols require specific instructions, specific surfaces, and specific environmental conditions. Any deviation from protocol undermines the test's reliability.

**What you want to hear:** "Yes, I've watched the footage. Here's what I noticed about the SFST administration..."

**If he deflects:** "I understand you may not have had time yet. When can I expect you'll have reviewed it? The footage may be subject to a retention policy."

**Pay attention to:** Whether Torres has watched the footage personally or delegated it to a paralegal, and whether he can describe specific observations.

---

**Q3:** "What is the prosecution's strongest piece of evidence, and what is our plan to address it?"

> This question, adapted from Tom Mesereau's reverse-engineering methodology, forces a concrete assessment. An attorney who understands the case can name the prosecution's best evidence and articulate a counter-strategy. An attorney who hasn't dug in will give a general answer.

**What you want to hear:** A specific answer, "Their strongest evidence is the breath test, and here's how we're addressing it..." or "The body camera footage shows X, and our approach is Y."

**If vague:** "Can you walk me through the top 3 pieces of evidence the prosecution has and how each one affects our position?"

### Motions & Deadlines

**Q6:** "Have you filed a discovery request under the Michael Morton Act? If yes, what has been produced? If no, what's the timeline?"

The Michael Morton Act (Tex. Code Crim. Proc. Art. 39.14) is Texas's open-file discovery statute. It's foundational, every other question depends on seeing the evidence.

**If yes → "What did the calibration records show?"**
**If no → "What is preventing the request, and when will it happen?"**

**Q7:** "What is the last date we can file pre-trial motions before Judge Alvarez? Are you considering a motion to suppress based on either the stop or the breath test?"

Two questions, one theme: timing. Motions filed after the deadline are waived, and the pre-trial hearing may be the critical checkpoint.

### Consequences

**Q8:** "If I accept the current plea offer, what specifically happens to my driver's license, and have you looked into an occupational license so I can still drive my children to school?"

This is your highest-stakes practical question. An occupational license (Tex. Transp. Code § 521.242) exists for exactly this situation. Torres should know the answer, and if he doesn't, that tells you something about the level of attention your personal circumstances have received.

**Q9:** "What are the full collateral consequences of a DWI conviction versus a reduced charge, specifically for employment background checks, insurance, and custody?"

> Most defendants focus on the sentence. The collateral consequences often last longer than any probation period. Background checks governed by the FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.) can surface a conviction for years. Insurance rate increases from a DWI (SR-22 requirement under Tex. Transp. Code § 601.051) typically last 2-3 years. A reduced charge may avoid some of these entirely.

### Evidence & Discovery

**Q10:** "What is the retention policy for body camera footage at the department that arrested me, and has a litigation hold been placed?"

Footage is evidence, and evidence can be destroyed if no one asks for it. A litigation hold prevents deletion. This should have been requested early in the case.

**If Torres hasn't requested it:** "Can you submit a preservation request this week? I'm concerned about the retention timeline."

---

**Q11:** "Have you obtained the breathalyzer operator's certification and training records? Was the certification current at the time of my test?"

An operator whose certification lapsed, even by days, creates a significant challenge to the reading's admissibility. McShane's forensic lawyer-scientist methodology treats this as a threshold question.

**Q12:**
> "Is there any evidence from my case that you haven't had time to review yet? I'd rather know now than be surprised later."

This is a direct but non-confrontational way to surface gaps. Most public defenders will be honest if you ask without judgment. The answer helps you understand the real status of your case, not the official status.

**Q13:** "If I were to seek a second opinion from a private DWI attorney, would you be open to that? Would it affect our working relationship?"

This question accomplishes two things: it tests whether Torres is secure enough in his work to welcome outside review, and it opens the door to a possibility you're entitled to exercise. Frame it as due diligence, not distrust.

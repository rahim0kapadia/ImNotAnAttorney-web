Understanding Your Charges

You told us you're charged with wire fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1343, in the Central District of California, following a grand jury indictment. The alleged amount is $180,000 in fraudulent invoices.

Under 18 U.S.C. § 1343, wire fraud requires the prosecution to prove each of the following elements:

Penalty range (18 U.S.C. § 1343 — federal felony): Up to 20 years in federal prison per count, fine up to $250,000, restitution, supervised release, and potential forfeiture. These are statutory maximums, not predictions. The questions in this report help you understand the realistic range for YOUR case. Federal sentencing is governed by the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines, which calculate a recommended range based on the specific facts — loss amount, role in the offense, acceptance of responsibility, and criminal history. What this means: Wire fraud is a serious federal charge, but "charged" does not mean "convicted." The prosecution must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt — including that YOU personally intended to defraud. In a multi-defendant case, the evidence against each defendant is evaluated separately. Your co-defendants' knowledge or intent doesn't automatically establish yours. That's the question your attorney should be analyzing.

Your Rights in This Process

  • Right to see all evidence (discovery): Under Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure Rule 16, you're entitled to disclosure of evidence the government intends to use, including documents, records, and electronic communications. Brady material (evidence favorable to you) must be disclosed under Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963).
  • Right to be consulted before any plea: Your attorney cannot accept or reject a plea offer without your knowledge and consent.
  • Right to understand your defense strategy: You're entitled to know not just that your attorney recommends a plea, but WHY — and what the alternative looks like.
  • Right to fire and replace your attorney: This is your right at any time, even mid-case. You're also entitled to fee arbitration through the state bar if there's a fee dispute.
  • Right to refuse a plea offer: No one can force you to plead guilty. The decision to plead guilty or go to trial is YOURS, not your attorney's.
  • Right to testify or not testify: Under the Fifth Amendment, you cannot be compelled to testify against yourself.

    6. When the Conversation Gets Difficult

    Scenario 1: "The evidence is strong — I really think the plea is the way to go."
  • What's happening: Your attorney may have a legitimate assessment of the evidence. But "the evidence is strong" is a conclusion, not an explanation.
  • What you can say: "I appreciate your assessment. It would help me to understand which specific pieces of evidence you believe are strongest against me personally — not against my co-defendants."
  • Why it works: You're asking for the analysis behind the recommendation, not rejecting it.
    • Scenario 2: "You could be looking at serious prison time if you go to trial."
  • What's happening: The federal trial penalty is real — defendants who go to trial and lose often receive significantly longer sentences than those who plead. But the question is whether this risk is being presented as information or as leverage.
  • What you can say: "I want to understand the full picture. What is the sentencing range if I plead versus if I'm convicted at trial? And what's the realistic probability of conviction based on the evidence against me specifically?"
  • Why it works: You're asking for a comparative analysis, not reacting emotionally to worst-case numbers.
    • Scenario 3: "Your co-defendants are cooperating — we have to move quickly."
  • What's happening: Co-defendant cooperation creates pressure, but it doesn't mean you're out of options. The question is what the cooperating defendants are saying about YOUR knowledge and role.
  • What you can say: "I understand there's time pressure. What specifically are my co-defendants saying about my involvement, and how does that change our defense options?"
  • Why it works: You're engaging with the urgency while gathering the information you need to make an informed decision.
    • Scenario 4: Won't explain the collateral consequences of the plea.
  • What's happening: The attorney may be focused on the criminal case and not the downstream impacts — professional licensing, debarment, tax implications, civil liability.
  • What you can say: "Before I make any decisions about the plea, I'd like to understand every consequence beyond the sentence itself — professional licenses, business restrictions, and restitution structure. Can we go through those specifically?"
  • Why it works: You've made it clear that the plea decision requires full information, not just the sentencing range.

    Q1. What specific evidence does the prosecution have that I personally knew about and intended to participate in the fraudulent invoices — as opposed to my partners doing this without my knowledge? (Golden Question — if you only ask one question, ask this one)

    Why it matters: Martin G. Weinberg, NACDL 2022 Lifetime Achievement Award recipient who won acquittals in the Varsity Blues case, builds white-collar defenses around the intent element. Wire fraud requires proof of specific intent to defraud — not just that fraud happened, but that YOU knowingly participated. In a case with multiple defendants, the prosecution must prove YOUR knowledge and intent independently. You told us you believe you were the only one who didn't know about the fraudulent invoices. If that's true, the prosecution's evidence against your co-defendants doesn't automatically become evidence against you. The question is what connects you specifically to the fraudulent conduct. Good answer: "Based on the discovery I've reviewed, the prosecution's case against you specifically rests on [these documents/communications/testimony]. Here's how I evaluate the strength of each piece..." If the answer is vague: "I understand the case as a whole may be strong. What I'm asking about is the evidence against ME individually — what proves I knew about the fraudulent invoices?" What to listen for: Whether the attorney can separate the evidence against you from the evidence against your co-defendants. If the answer is "the evidence against the group is strong," the individual analysis may not have been done yet.

    Q2. Has the digital evidence been reviewed for what it shows about my individual role — specifically, what emails, documents, or communications connect me to the fraudulent invoices?

    Why it matters: Cristina C. Arguedas, Trial Lawyers Hall of Fame inductee who won a "factually innocent" verdict in U.S. v. FedEx, focuses on pre-indictment intervention and document analysis. In a wire fraud case, the prosecution's case is built on digital communications — emails, invoices, wire transfers. The question is which of those communications involve you, and what they actually show about your knowledge. You told us digital evidence exists. Whether that evidence proves your involvement or your innocence depends on what it actually says. Good answer: "I've reviewed the digital evidence. Here are the specific communications that the prosecution may use against you, and here's what they actually show..." If the answer is vague: "Which specific emails or documents does the prosecution have that connect me to the fraudulent invoices — not the business generally, but the fraud specifically?" What to listen for: Whether the attorney has done a document-by-document analysis of what connects you to the alleged fraud, or is evaluating the case as a whole.

    Q3. You've recommended the plea — can you walk me through what a trial defense would look like based on my position that I didn't know about the fraudulent invoices?

    Why it matters: Before accepting any plea, you're entitled to understand what the alternative looks like. A defense based on lack of knowledge — that you were a business owner who didn't know her partners were creating fraudulent invoices — is a viable theory. The question is whether your attorney has evaluated what that defense would look like at trial: what evidence supports it, what evidence undermines it, and what the realistic chances are. When an attorney recommends a plea without discussing the defense alternative, it's worth understanding whether the recommendation is based on a full analysis or a general assessment of case strength. Good answer: "If we went to trial, the defense would look like this: [specific theory]. The strengths are [X]. The risks are [Y]. Here's why I believe the plea is the better option..." If the answer is vague: "I want to understand both paths before making a decision. What would the jury hear in our defense if we went to trial?" What to listen for: Whether the attorney has analyzed a trial scenario at all. If the defense has never been sketched out, the plea recommendation may not be based on a complete evaluation.

    Q4. What are my co-defendants' cooperation statuses — are either of them cooperating with the government, and if so, what are they saying about my involvement?

    Why it matters: David B. Smith, author of Prosecution and Defense of Forfeiture Cases, emphasizes that in multi-defendant cases, understanding each co-defendant's posture is essential. If your business partners are cooperating with the prosecution, what they say about your knowledge and role directly affects your case. Cooperation can cut both ways — they may implicate you to reduce their own exposure, or their statements may actually support your position that you didn't know about the fraud. Good answer: "Here's what I know about your co-defendants' cooperation status and what they've said about your role..." If the answer is vague: "Are either of my co-defendants cooperating, and has the prosecution disclosed what they're saying about me?" What to listen for: Whether the attorney is tracking the co-defendants' cases actively or treating your case in isolation.

    Q5. What is my preliminary sentencing guidelines calculation — and what factors could move it up or down?

    Why it matters: Federal sentencing is governed by the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines (USSG). The recommended range depends on the loss amount, your role in the offense, acceptance of responsibility, and criminal history. For $180,000 in alleged loss, the base offense level for wire fraud under USSG §2B1.1 starts at 7, with enhancements based on loss amount. Understanding the preliminary calculation — and what factors might reduce it — is essential before evaluating any plea offer. Martin Weinberg's methodology includes challenging the loss amount calculation, arguing for minor role adjustment (USSG §3B1.2), and establishing good faith reliance on professional advice as an intent defense. Good answer: "Based on the loss amount and your role, the preliminary guidelines range is [X-Y months]. Here's what could bring it down: minor role adjustment, acceptance of responsibility, cooperation credit..." If the answer is vague: "I'd like to see the preliminary guidelines worksheet for my case. Can we go through each factor?" What to listen for: Whether the attorney has done a preliminary guidelines calculation or is speaking in general terms about sentencing.

    Q6. What would the full collateral consequences of a guilty plea look like — professional licenses, business operations, debarment, and civil liability?

    Why it matters: You specifically asked about professional licenses and running a business. A federal wire fraud conviction can trigger: debarment from government contracts (FAR 9.406-2), restrictions on serving as a corporate officer or director (SOX § 305 for SEC-regulated entities, 15 U.S.C. § 7244), state professional licensing actions, FCRA-governed background check disclosures (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.), and potential civil liability from the alleged fraud victims. The restitution alone — $180,000 — could be financially devastating. These consequences often exceed the prison sentence in long-term impact. What to listen for: Whether the attorney addresses collateral consequences as part of the plea evaluation or treats them as separate issues.

    Q7. Have you considered filing any pretrial motions — specifically related to my individual role, the evidence against me personally, or discovery issues?

    Why it matters: Federal pretrial motions can include motions to sever (separate your trial from your co-defendants'), motions to suppress evidence, and motions for bill of particulars (requiring the prosecution to specify exactly what YOU allegedly did). In a multi-defendant case, severance can be particularly important if your defense — "I didn't know" — is fundamentally different from your co-defendants'. What to listen for: Whether the attorney has evaluated motions specific to your situation or is treating the case as a group.

    ---

    Q8. Is severance (separating my trial from my co-defendants') something we should consider, given that my defense is fundamentally different from theirs?

    Why it matters: Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 14, the court can sever a defendant's trial if joinder would cause prejudice. If your co-defendants' defense is "we did it but here's why the sentence should be lighter" and your defense is "I didn't know," being tried together could be prejudicial. The jury may associate you with your co-defendants' admitted conduct. What to listen for: Whether the attorney has considered severance or assumed the cases will proceed together.

    ---

    Q9. What is the federal trial penalty — and how does it factor into the plea recommendation?

    Why it matters: The "trial penalty" refers to the significantly longer sentences defendants often receive after losing at trial compared to what they would have received under a plea agreement. This is a real dynamic in federal court — defendants who go to trial can lose the "acceptance of responsibility" credit (a 2-3 level reduction under USSG §3E1.1), and prosecutors may recommend higher sentences. Understanding this dynamic helps you evaluate the plea offer with clear eyes. What to listen for: Whether the attorney frames the trial penalty as information or as pressure. Both the risk and the benefit of going to trial should be part of the analysis.

    ---

    Q10. Has full discovery been produced, and have you reviewed the evidence that specifically relates to my individual conduct?

    Why it matters: Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 16 and Brady v. Maryland, the government must disclose evidence — including evidence favorable to you. In a multi-defendant fraud case, the discovery can be voluminous. The question is whether your attorney has reviewed the portions that relate to YOUR specific role and conduct, not just the case as a whole. What to listen for: Whether the attorney can discuss specific documents related to your individual involvement.

    ---

    Q11. If I accept the plea, what happens to the $180,000 restitution — is it joint and several with my co-defendants, or am I responsible for the full amount individually?

    Why it matters: In federal multi-defendant cases, restitution is often imposed jointly and severally (each person can be held responsible for the full amount, not just their share). Under 18 U.S.C. § 3664, the court determines the restitution structure. If you're held jointly and severally liable for $180,000 and your co-defendants can't pay, you may bear the entire amount. Understanding the restitution structure is critical before accepting any plea. What to listen for: Whether the attorney has evaluated the restitution structure or is focused only on the prison term.

    ---

    Q12. What happens at my next court date on May 15 — what type of hearing is it, and what decisions will be made?

    Why it matters: Knowing what to expect reduces anxiety. Your May 15 hearing may be a status conference, a change-of-plea hearing (if you decide to accept the plea), or a pretrial conference. Understanding which it is — and whether any decisions need to be made before then — helps you prepare. What to listen for: A clear explanation of the hearing type and what's expected of you.

    ---

    Q13. Is there any evidence that supports my position — emails, communications, or records showing I wasn't involved in creating the fraudulent invoices?

    Why it matters: Cristina Arguedas's methodology includes identifying evidence that the government has but may not be highlighting — evidence that supports your defense. In a wire fraud case built on documents, there may be communications that show you asking genuine questions about invoices, expressing surprise at certain transactions, or being excluded from discussions about the fraudulent conduct. This evidence may be in the government's possession under Brady obligations. What to listen for: Whether the attorney has looked for exculpatory evidence or focused only on evaluating the prosecution's case.

    ---

    Q14. Has the government made any cooperation or proffer offers — and what would that process look like if I'm interested?

    Why it matters: In federal cases, cooperation (providing information to the government) can result in a substantial assistance departure under USSG §5K1.1 — a significant sentence reduction. However, proffer sessions (meetings where you share information with prosecutors, usually under a "proffer agreement" that provides limited protections) carry risks. What you say can be used against you under certain circumstances. Understanding whether this option exists — and its risks — is part of an informed decision. What to listen for: Whether the attorney has explored this avenue and can explain the risks and benefits clearly.

    ---

    Q15. If I'm convicted — whether by plea or trial — what are the appeal options, and what would post-conviction look like?

    Why it matters: Understanding the full trajectory — not just the next hearing — helps you plan. Federal appeals are filed in the Circuit Court of Appeals. Post-conviction options include motions under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. The timeline for these options depends on whether you plead or go to trial. What to listen for: Whether the attorney has thought about the case beyond the immediate plea decision.

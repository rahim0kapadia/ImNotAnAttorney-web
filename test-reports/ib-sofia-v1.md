### 3b. Defense Theories

**Self-Defense (Branca framework)**
Andrew Branca's five-element analysis of justifiable force applies directly: (1) innocence — you didn't initiate the physical confrontation, (2) imminence — the threat was immediate, (3) proportionality — your response matched the threat level, (4) avoidance — retreat wasn't safely available, and (5) reasonableness — a reasonable person in your situation would have responded similarly. Texas law does not require retreat in your own home (Castle Doctrine, Tex. Penal Code § 9.32). The question for Chen: "Based on what I've told you about the incident, do the facts support a self-defense claim?"

Robert Tayac's framework for domestic defense emphasizes that domestic situations involve complex relationship dynamics. The complaining witness's account is one perspective — the officer's recording, 911 call, physical evidence, and your account provide additional context. If the evidence contradicts the initial report, that creates reasonable doubt.

Domestic-related cases with a reluctant or non-cooperative complaining witness present distinct challenges for the prosecution. Without the witness's testimony, the state must prove its case through other evidence — the officer's recording, medical records, and physical evidence. If that evidence is ambiguous or supports the defense, the case weakens significantly.

## Appendix D: Targeted Follow-Up Questions

These questions fill the specific gaps in what you know. Each is calibrated to elicit a substantive answer.

### Case Strategy

**Q1 (Golden Question):** "Based on the police report and any evidence you've reviewed, what is your initial read on the strength of the state's case and the defense options?"

Why this is urgent: After 2 months, this answer is foundational. Robert Tayac's framework for domestic defense begins with evaluating all the evidence — not just the complaining witness's account. Chen's "we'll see what the DA offers" may reflect genuine strategy in progress — but you're entitled to know her initial assessment of the evidence.

What you want to hear: She describes the state's case with specifics — what the police report says, whether the footage has been reviewed, and what defense theories the evidence supports.

If she's vague: "Could you identify the 3-5 most important pieces of evidence and tell me what they show about what happened?"

The key signal: Specific evidence with analysis of what it means for the defense — not generalities about cases like yours.

---

**Q2:** "I've had difficulty reaching you consistently. What's the most reliable way to communicate — email, phone, in-person meetings, a paralegal contact?"

This solves the communication challenge rather than assigning blame. Public defenders manage heavy caseloads — establishing a reliable communication channel is practical, not personal. A strong response: a specific method and expected response time. If vague: "Could you name the single best way to reach you and a realistic timeline for responses?"

### Evidence Review

**Q3:** "Have you reviewed the body camera footage and 911 recording? Do they support or contradict a self-defense claim?"

This is the most important evidence question. The officer's recording captures the immediate scene — positioning, injuries, demeanor, and statements from both parties. The 911 recording captures the caller's account but not what happened before the call. If Chen hasn't reviewed them: "When do you expect to review the footage? Could we discuss it before the hearing?"

**Q4:** "Do I have photographs or medical records documenting injuries I sustained? How do we preserve and present that evidence?"

| She's already identified your injuries as relevant evidence | "I didn't know you were injured" |

**Q5:** "Is self-defense viable based on what you know? What specific facts support or undermine it?"

Andrew Branca's five-element framework applies: innocence (who initiated), imminence (immediate threat), proportionality (response matched threat), avoidance (retreat wasn't safe — Castle Doctrine may apply), and reasonableness (what a reasonable person would do). A good answer addresses each element specifically, not generically.

### Consequences

**Q12 + Q13** (pair these — the second follows naturally from the first):

Then: "How does each outcome affect my custody situation, the protective order, and the firearms prohibition?"

A calibrated range helps you decide with data instead of fear or hope. In cases like yours, the collateral consequences (custody, gun rights, enhancement) often matter more than the direct criminal penalty. Understanding the full impact of each option — not just jail time — is essential.

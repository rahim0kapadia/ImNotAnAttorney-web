    3b. Defense Theories

    These are established categories of defense inquiry for near-limit cases. Your attorney determines which apply to your specific facts.

    Instrument Precision Challenge (Taylor/McShane methodology)

    Breathalyzers have documented margins of error — typically ±0.005 to ±0.02. A 0.09 reading on an instrument with a ±0.01 margin means the true level could fall from 0.08 to 0.10. At 0.08, you're at the legal limit — not above it. Applicability depends on the specific model, calibration history, and operator certification.

    Rising BAC Defense

    Blood alcohol continues rising after the last drink. If there was a meaningful delay between driving and the breath test, the actual level while driving may have been below 0.08. This is a timeline question Torres can map.

    Field Sobriety Test Validity (Head methodology)

    William "Bubba" Head's approach examines whether standardized field sobriety tests (SFSTs) were administered per National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) protocol. Deviations — wrong instructions, uneven surface, inadequate demonstration — undermine reliability. Body camera footage is the critical evidence here.

    Traffic Stop Legality

    Every DWI case begins with a traffic stop. If the officer lacked reasonable suspicion, a motion to suppress can exclude everything that followed — including the breath test.

    Appendix A: Brady/Giglio Checklist

    What This Is: Under Brady v. Maryland (1963) and Giglio v. United States (1972), the prosecution is constitutionally required to disclose evidence that is favorable to the defense. This includes exculpatory evidence (Brady) and impeachment evidence (Giglio). Ask your attorney: "Have you received all Brady/Giglio material? Is there anything outstanding?"

    Evidence the Prosecution Must Disclose:

  • ☐ Exculpatory evidence (anything suggesting innocence)
  • ☐ Impeachment evidence (anything undermining prosecution witnesses)
  • ☐ Prior inconsistent statements by witnesses
  • ☐ Deals, promises, or inducements to witnesses
  • ☐ Criminal records of prosecution witnesses
  • ☐ Evidence of witness bias or motive to lie
  • ☐ Lab reports, forensic analysis, chain of custody documentation
  • ☐ Surveillance footage, body camera footage, dashcam footage
  • ☐ 911 calls and dispatch records
  • ☐ Prior complaints against arresting officers
  • ☐ Internal affairs investigations of involved officers
  • ☐ Evidence contradicting the prosecution's theory

    • What to Ask Your Attorney:

  • "Have you filed a specific Brady demand or are you relying on the general obligation?"
  • "Is there a standing discovery order in this case?"
  • "Have you received all police reports, including supplemental reports?"
  • "Are there any witnesses the prosecution hasn't disclosed?"

  • The judge will typically pass your case and come back to it.
  • Do not try to represent yourself or discuss case details.
  • After the hearing, document what happened and send a written summary.

    Appendix C: Your Rights During Criminal Proceedings

    These rights exist regardless of your charge, your attorney, or your county.

    Constitutional Rights:

  • Right to remain silent (5th Amendment) — You cannot be compelled to testify against yourself
  • Right to an attorney (6th Amendment) — If you cannot afford one, one will be appointed
  • Right to a speedy trial (6th Amendment) — Timelines vary by state and jurisdiction
  • Right to confront witnesses (6th Amendment) — You can cross-examine anyone who testifies against you
  • Right against unreasonable search and seizure (4th Amendment) — Evidence obtained illegally may be suppressed
  • Right to a jury trial (6th Amendment) — For serious offenses, you have the right to be judged by a jury of your peers
  • Right to due process (14th Amendment) — Fair procedures must be followed
  • Right against double jeopardy (5th Amendment) — You cannot be tried twice for the same offense
  • Right to be presumed innocent — The prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt

    • Your Rights With Your Attorney:

  • You have the right to know what is happening in your case at all times
  • You have the right to be consulted before major decisions are made
  • You have the right to make the final decision on whether to accept a plea or go to trial
  • You have the right to effective assistance of counsel (Strickland v. Washington)
  • You have the right to change attorneys at any time (though timing and procedural requirements apply)

    • If You Feel Your Rights Are Being Violated:

  • Document everything in writing (dates, times, what was said)
  • Follow the Advocacy Steps in Section 6 of this brief
  • Contact your state bar association's client protection hotline

    • Appendix D: Targeted Follow-Up Questions

    These questions fill the specific gaps in what you know. Each is calibrated to elicit a substantive answer.

    Case Strategy

    Q1 (Golden Question): "You mentioned my BAC is "borderline" and there might be options — can you walk me through what those options are and which ones apply to my case?"

    Why this is urgent: After 3 months, this answer is overdue. Lawrence Taylor's DWI defense methodology identifies near-limit cases as having the widest range of defense options — but only if investigated. Torres flagged the issue; now you need specifics.

    What you want to hear: He names 2-3 specific theories (instrument margin of error, rising blood alcohol, SFST protocol) and explains which he's investigated or plans to investigate.

    If he's vague: "Could you narrow it to the top two options and what information you'd need to evaluate them?"

    The key signal: Specific theories with a factual basis — not generalities. Note each option and include them in your summary email.

    Q2: "I've called four times in six weeks and received one callback. What's the most reliable way to reach you — email, portal, scheduled calls?"

    This solves the communication problem rather than assigning blame — what Chris Voss calls a "calibrated question." A strong response: a specific method and expected response time. If vague: "Could you name the single best channel to reach you?" Pay attention to whether he gives a concrete plan vs. reassurance.

    Evidence & Discovery

    Q3: "Have you obtained and reviewed the body camera footage from my traffic stop?"

    William "Bubba" Head's methodology begins with footage review — it provides objective documentation of the encounter.

  • If "Yes, I've watched it" → Ask what he noticed. This is the gold standard response.
  • If "I've requested it but haven't reviewed it yet" → Ask when he expects to review it and whether it will happen before the hearing.
  • If "I haven't requested it" → "Can we request it now? Retention policies vary — footage can be deleted." This is time-sensitive.

    • Whether footage has been obtained AND reviewed — not just requested — tells you where preparation stands.

    Q4: "What is the margin of error for the breathalyzer model used in my test, and does my 0.09 reading fall within that margin?"

    Justin McShane's forensic methodology focuses on instrument precision. Most breathalyzers have a margin of ±0.005 to ±0.02 — meaning a 0.09 could represent a true level from 0.07 to 0.11. A good answer identifies the specific model, margin, and last calibration date. If he doesn't know, that's a discovery item to follow up on.

    Q5: "I'm a registered nurse and sole provider for two children. How would a DWI conviction versus a reduction versus a dismissal through diversion affect my nursing license?"

    Under Texas Occupations Code § 301.452, the Board of Nursing reviews criminal convictions. The difference between outcomes may determine whether the Board acts — career-defining territory. Listen for whether the nursing license has been factored into strategy, not just the criminal outcome. If not: "Could you connect me with someone experienced with professional licensing consequences?"

    Q7 + Q8 (ask together — they're related):

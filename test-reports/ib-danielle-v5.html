    3b. Defense Theories

    Based on Justin McShane's methodology — he was the first attorney designated a "Forensic Lawyer-Scientist" by the American Chemical Society — the following theories apply to a near-threshold reading like yours:

    1. Instrument Precision Challenge

    Every breathalyzer has a documented margin of error. If the instrument used at your stop has a ±0.01 margin, your true BAC could have been anywhere from 0.08 to 0.10. At 0.08, the reading is not above the legal limit — it IS the legal limit, and margin-of-error arguments become compelling. McShane's framework: obtain the specific instrument's calibration records, maintenance logs, and the operator's certification history. If any calibration was overdue or the operator's certification had lapsed, the reading's reliability is directly challenged.

    2. Rising BAC Defense

    If you consumed alcohol shortly before driving, your blood alcohol concentration may still have been rising at the time of the stop. The reading at the station could reflect a level higher than what it was while you were actually driving. The question for Torres: "What was the time gap between the stop and the breath test, and does a rising BAC argument apply?"

    3. Traffic Stop Legality

    Under the Fourth Amendment and the Texas Constitution (Art. I, § 9), the initial traffic stop must have been supported by reasonable suspicion. If the stop itself was improper, everything that followed — field sobriety tests, the breath test, arrest — becomes potentially suppressible. This is the foundation of Lawrence Taylor's methodology: challenge every procedural step, starting with the stop.

    4. Field Sobriety Test Challenge

    You told us field sobriety tests were performed. Standardized field sobriety tests (SFSTs) have well-documented limitations. NHTSA's own research shows that even properly administered SFSTs have accuracy rates below 80% for the one-leg stand and walk-and-turn. William "Bubba" Head's methodology focuses on identifying administration errors — wrong instructions, improper surface, environmental factors — that further reduce reliability.

    Appendix A: Brady/Giglio Checklist

    What This Is: Under Brady v. Maryland (1963) and Giglio v. United States (1972), the prosecution is constitutionally required to disclose evidence that is favorable to the defense. This includes exculpatory evidence (Brady) and impeachment evidence (Giglio). Ask your attorney: "Have you received all Brady/Giglio material? Is there anything outstanding?"

    Evidence the Prosecution Must Disclose:

  • ☐ Exculpatory evidence (anything suggesting innocence)
  • ☐ Impeachment evidence (anything undermining prosecution witnesses)
  • ☐ Prior inconsistent statements by witnesses
  • ☐ Deals, promises, or inducements to witnesses
  • ☐ Criminal records of prosecution witnesses
  • ☐ Evidence of witness bias or motive to lie
  • ☐ Lab reports, forensic analysis, chain of custody documentation
  • ☐ Surveillance footage, body camera footage, dashcam footage
  • ☐ 911 calls and dispatch records
  • ☐ Prior complaints against arresting officers
  • ☐ Internal affairs investigations of involved officers
  • ☐ Evidence contradicting the prosecution's theory

    • What to Ask Your Attorney:

  • "Have you filed a specific Brady demand or are you relying on the general obligation?"
  • "Is there a standing discovery order in this case?"
  • "Have you received all police reports, including supplemental reports?"
  • "Are there any witnesses the prosecution hasn't disclosed?"

    Appendix C: Your Rights During Criminal Proceedings

    These rights exist regardless of your charge, your attorney, or your county.

    Constitutional Rights:

  • Right to remain silent (5th Amendment) — You cannot be compelled to testify against yourself
  • Right to an attorney (6th Amendment) — If you cannot afford one, one will be appointed
  • Right to a speedy trial (6th Amendment) — Timelines vary by state and jurisdiction
  • Right to confront witnesses (6th Amendment) — You can cross-examine anyone who testifies against you
  • Right against unreasonable search and seizure (4th Amendment) — Evidence obtained illegally may be suppressed
  • Right to a jury trial (6th Amendment) — For serious offenses, you have the right to be judged by a jury of your peers
  • Right to due process (14th Amendment) — Fair procedures must be followed
  • Right against double jeopardy (5th Amendment) — You cannot be tried twice for the same offense
  • Right to be presumed innocent — The prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt

    • Your Rights With Your Attorney:

  • You have the right to know what is happening in your case at all times
  • You have the right to be consulted before major decisions are made
  • You have the right to make the final decision on whether to accept a plea or go to trial
  • You have the right to effective assistance of counsel (Strickland v. Washington)
  • You have the right to change attorneys at any time (though timing and procedural requirements apply)

    • If You Feel Your Rights Are Being Violated:

  • Document everything in writing (dates, times, what was said)
  • Follow the Advocacy Steps in Section 6 of this brief
  • Contact your state bar association's client protection hotline

    Case Strategy

    Q1 — The Golden Question (if you only ask one, ask this one):

    "You said my BAC of 0.09 is borderline. Have you identified the specific instrument used, pulled its calibration history, and determined whether the margin of error creates a viable defense — or are you still working on that?"

    Why it matters: Justin McShane's framework for challenging near-threshold readings starts with one question: is this instrument reliable enough to distinguish 0.08 from 0.09? The answer lives in the calibration records, not the reading itself. A strong response: Torres can name the instrument model, has reviewed or requested calibration records, and can articulate whether precision is a viable defense theory. If he seems unsure: "When do you expect to have the calibration records? I want to understand the timeline for this part of the defense." The key signal: Whether Torres is building a case around the reading's reliability or treating the 0.09 as a settled fact. Those are two different defense postures.

    ---

    Q2: "Have you personally reviewed the body camera footage from my stop, and if so, did the officer follow SFST administration protocols correctly?" Background: William "Bubba" Head's methodology centers on identifying administration errors in field sobriety tests. NHTSA protocols require specific instructions, specific surfaces, and specific environmental conditions. Any deviation from protocol undermines the test's reliability. What you want to hear: "Yes, I've watched the footage. Here's what I noticed about the SFST administration..." If he deflects: "I understand you may not have had time yet. When can I expect you'll have reviewed it? The footage may be subject to a retention policy." Pay attention to: Whether Torres has watched the footage personally or delegated it to a paralegal — and whether he can describe specific observations.

    ---

    Q3: "What is the prosecution's strongest piece of evidence, and what is our plan to address it?"
    This question, adapted from Tom Mesereau's reverse-engineering methodology, forces a concrete assessment. An attorney who understands the case can name the prosecution's best evidence and articulate a counter-strategy. An attorney who hasn't dug in will give a general answer.
    What you want to hear: A specific answer — "Their strongest evidence is the breath test, and here's how we're addressing it..." or "The body camera footage shows X, and our approach is Y." If vague: "Can you walk me through the top 3 pieces of evidence the prosecution has and how each one affects our position?"

    Consequences

    Q8: "If I accept the current plea offer, what specifically happens to my driver's license, and have you looked into an occupational license so I can still drive my children to school?"

    This is your highest-stakes practical question. An occupational license (Tex. Transp. Code § 521.242) exists for exactly this situation. Torres should know the answer — and if he doesn't, that tells you something about the level of attention your personal circumstances have received.

    ---

    Q9: "What are the full collateral consequences of a DWI conviction versus a reduced charge — specifically for employment background checks, insurance, and custody?"
    Most defendants focus on the sentence. The collateral consequences often last longer than any probation period. Background checks governed by the FCRA (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.) can surface a conviction for years. Insurance rate increases from a DWI (SR-22 requirement under Tex. Transp. Code § 601.051) typically last 2-3 years. A reduced charge may avoid some of these entirely.

    Evidence & Discovery

    Q10: "What is the retention policy for body camera footage at the department that arrested me, and has a litigation hold been placed?"

    Footage is evidence — and evidence can be destroyed if no one asks for it. A litigation hold prevents deletion. This should have been requested early in the case.

    If Torres hasn't requested it: "Can you submit a preservation request this week? I'm concerned about the retention timeline."

    Q11: "Have you obtained the breathalyzer operator's certification and training records? Was the certification current at the time of my test?"

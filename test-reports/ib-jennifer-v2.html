    3b. Defense Theories

    Based on your intake, three viable defense theories emerge:

    1. Good-faith belief / Lack of specific intent (primary)

    You told us you believed the transactions were legitimate. Wire fraud under § 1343 requires proof of specific intent — that you knowingly devised or participated in a scheme to defraud. If your emails, communications, and financial records show you processed transactions in the ordinary course of business as CFO, the government must prove you knew the underlying business was fraudulent. This is the distinction between a CFO who facilitates fraud and a CFO doing her job. Defense attorneys like Ellen Yaroshefsky emphasize that "the government loves to point at the person who signed the checks — but signing checks is what CFOs do."

    2. Reliance on others / Advice of counsel or superiors

    If the CEO directed the transactions and represented them as legitimate business, your reliance on those representations is relevant. This isn't a complete defense on its own, but it contextualizes your role. The question for Park: "Can we establish that I was following directives from the CEO, and does that reduce my culpability under the guidelines?"

    3. Severance

    With three defendants in the indictment, a joint trial creates spillover risk — the jury hears evidence about the CEO's and VP's conduct and attributes it to you. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 14, you can move to sever your trial if a joint trial would be prejudicial. The viability depends on how intertwined the government's evidence is.

    Section 5: Protecting Your Case and Life

    5a. Protecting Your Case

    Do not discuss your case with anyone except David Park — including the CEO and VP.

    This is not generic advice. In multi-defendant federal cases, conversations between the accused are a primary source of government evidence. If the CEO or VP is working with the government, any communication you have with them may be reported. The same applies to mutual friends, former colleagues, and family members who might be contacted by investigators.

    Specific protections:
  • Social media: Deactivate or lock all accounts. Federal investigators routinely review social media for evidence of lifestyle, travel, asset concealment, and communications about the case. Anything you post can appear in a courtroom.
  • Financial activity: Do not move, transfer, or conceal assets. The government is watching your financial activity. Any unusual transactions will be characterized as consciousness of guilt — hiding money from seizure. Even legitimate transactions (selling a car, paying down debt) can be misinterpreted.
  • Work emails and devices: If you retained any work devices or have access to work email accounts, do not access, delete, or modify anything. Spoliation of evidence in a federal case can result in additional charges (obstruction of justice, 18 U.S.C. § 1519).

    Appendix A: Brady/Giglio Checklist

    What This Is: Under Brady v. Maryland (1963) and Giglio v. United States (1972), the prosecution is constitutionally required to disclose evidence that is favorable to the defense. This includes exculpatory evidence (Brady) and impeachment evidence (Giglio). Ask your attorney: "Have you received all Brady/Giglio material? Is there anything outstanding?"

    Evidence the Prosecution Must Disclose:

  • ☐ Exculpatory evidence (anything suggesting innocence)
  • ☐ Impeachment evidence (anything undermining prosecution witnesses)
  • ☐ Prior inconsistent statements by witnesses
  • ☐ Deals, promises, or inducements to witnesses
  • ☐ Criminal records of prosecution witnesses
  • ☐ Evidence of witness bias or motive to lie
  • ☐ Lab reports, forensic analysis, chain of custody documentation
  • ☐ Surveillance footage, body camera footage, dashcam footage
  • ☐ 911 calls and dispatch records
  • ☐ Prior complaints against arresting officers
  • ☐ Internal affairs investigations of involved officers
  • ☐ Evidence contradicting the prosecution's theory

    • What to Ask Your Attorney:

  • "Have you filed a specific Brady demand or are you relying on the general obligation?"
  • "Is there a standing discovery order in this case?"
  • "Have you received all police reports, including supplemental reports?"
  • "Are there any witnesses the prosecution hasn't disclosed?"

    Appendix C: Your Rights During Criminal Proceedings

    These rights exist regardless of your charge, your attorney, or your county.

    Constitutional Rights:

  • Right to remain silent (5th Amendment) — You cannot be compelled to testify against yourself
  • Right to an attorney (6th Amendment) — If you cannot afford one, one will be appointed
  • Right to a speedy trial (6th Amendment) — Timelines vary by state and jurisdiction
  • Right to confront witnesses (6th Amendment) — You can cross-examine anyone who testifies against you
  • Right against unreasonable search and seizure (4th Amendment) — Evidence obtained illegally may be suppressed
  • Right to a jury trial (6th Amendment) — For serious offenses, you have the right to be judged by a jury of your peers
  • Right to due process (14th Amendment) — Fair procedures must be followed
  • Right against double jeopardy (5th Amendment) — You cannot be tried twice for the same offense
  • Right to be presumed innocent — The prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt

    • Your Rights With Your Attorney:

  • You have the right to know what is happening in your case at all times
  • You have the right to be consulted before major decisions are made
  • You have the right to make the final decision on whether to accept a plea or go to trial
  • You have the right to effective assistance of counsel (Strickland v. Washington)
  • You have the right to change attorneys at any time (though timing and procedural requirements apply)

    • If You Feel Your Rights Are Being Violated:

  • Document everything in writing (dates, times, what was said)
  • Follow the Advocacy Steps in Section 6 of this brief
  • Contact your state bar association's client protection hotline

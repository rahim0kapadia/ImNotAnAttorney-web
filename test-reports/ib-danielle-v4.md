### 3b. Defense Theories

These are established categories of defense inquiry for near-limit cases. Your attorney determines which apply to your specific facts.

**Instrument Precision Challenge (Taylor/McShane methodology)**
Breathalyzers have documented margins of error — typically ±0.005 to ±0.02. A 0.09 reading on an instrument with a ±0.01 margin means the true level could fall from 0.08 to 0.10. At 0.08, you're at the legal limit — not above it. Applicability depends on the specific model, calibration history, and operator certification.

**Rising BAC Defense**
Blood alcohol continues rising after the last drink. If there was a meaningful delay between driving and the breath test, the actual level while driving may have been below 0.08. This is a timeline question Torres can map.

**Field Sobriety Test Validity (Head methodology)**
William "Bubba" Head's approach examines whether standardized field sobriety tests (SFSTs) were administered per National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) protocol. Deviations — wrong instructions, uneven surface, inadequate demonstration — undermine reliability. Body camera footage is the critical evidence here.

**Traffic Stop Legality**
Every DWI case begins with a traffic stop. If the officer lacked reasonable suspicion, a motion to suppress can exclude everything that followed — including the breath test.

**Q4:** "What is the margin of error for the breathalyzer model used in my test, and does my 0.09 reading fall within that margin?"

Justin McShane's forensic methodology focuses on instrument precision. Most breathalyzers have a margin of ±0.005 to ±0.02 — meaning a 0.09 could represent a true level from 0.07 to 0.11. A good answer identifies the specific model, margin, and last calibration date. If he doesn't know, that's a discovery item to follow up on.

**Q5:** "I'm a registered nurse and sole provider for two children. How would a DWI conviction versus a reduction versus a dismissal through diversion affect my nursing license?"

Under Texas Occupations Code § 301.452, the Board of Nursing reviews criminal convictions. The difference between outcomes may determine whether the Board acts — career-defining territory. Listen for whether the nursing license has been factored into strategy, not just the criminal outcome. If not: "Could you connect me with someone experienced with professional licensing consequences?"

### Motions & Deadlines

**Q7 + Q8** (ask together — they're related):

"Are there any motions — suppression, discovery compulsion — that we should consider filing before April 15?" AND "Was an ALR hearing requested within the 15-day window after my arrest?"

Both are deadline-sensitive. Pre-trial motions are generally filed before the hearing, and the ALR (Administrative License Revocation) process runs on a completely separate track with its own 15-day window from arrest. If he answers Q7 with "I haven't looked into it," ask Q8 immediately — it tests whether time-sensitive items are being tracked. A clear yes or no on the ALR is factual. If vague: "Can you check? The ALR is separate from the criminal case."

### Plea Evaluation

**Q9:** "Is the current plea offer the standard first offer, or has it been negotiated based on my situation?"

- **If yes, it's standard →** "What would a negotiated offer look like, and what would we need to get there?"
- **If it's been negotiated →** "What did the original offer look like, and what moved the needle?"
- **If he doesn't know →** That's an answer too. Standard offers are starting positions, not final ones.

**Q10:** "Does Harris County offer pre-trial diversion for first-time DWI? Would I qualify?"

Diversion could mean dismissal — which matters enormously for your nursing license. Texas prohibits deferred adjudication for DWI (Art. 42A.102), but diversion is a separate mechanism. If he hasn't investigated: "Could you find out? Dismissal vs. conviction is the difference for my license."

### Consequences

**Q12 + Q13** (pair these — the second follows naturally from the first):

First ask: "What are the realistic best-case and worst-case outcomes, given everything you know right now?"
Then: "If I don't accept the plea, what happens? Does the offer get worse, stay the same, or is there room?"

A calibrated range helps you decide with data instead of fear or hope. The second question tests whether the current offer is a ceiling or a floor. Follow-up: "Have you seen cases where the offer improved after motions were filed?"

**Q14:** "Were the field sobriety tests recorded, and were they administered per NHTSA standards?"

| What to listen for | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| "Yes, I've reviewed the footage" | He's doing his job — good sign |
| "I've only read the police report" | Reports omit officer errors. Footage is the objective record. |
| "I don't think they were recorded" | Dash cam and body cam policies vary. Worth confirming. |

**Q15:** "Is there anything about my case that concerns you or that I should know?"

This open-ended question invites information attorneys might not volunteer. Candor — even if it includes concerns — is what enables informed decisions.

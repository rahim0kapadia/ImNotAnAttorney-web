    3b. Defense Theories

    These are established categories of defense inquiry for borderline-BAC DWI cases. Your attorney can determine which apply to your facts.

    Instrument Precision Challenge (Taylor/McShane methodology)

    Breathalyzers are measurement instruments with a documented margin of error — typically ±0.005 to ±0.02. A 0.09 reading on an instrument with a ±0.01 margin means the true BAC could be anywhere from 0.08 to 0.10. At 0.08, you're at the legal limit — not above it. Whether this defense applies depends on the specific breathalyzer model, its calibration history, and the operator's certification. These are facts in the discovery file that your attorney can request and review.

    Rising BAC Defense

    BAC continues to rise after the last drink is consumed. If there was a meaningful delay between when you were driving and when the breath test was administered, your BAC at the time of driving may have been below 0.08. This is a factual question based on the timeline — when did you last drink, when were you stopped, and when was the test administered?

    Field Sobriety Test Validity (Head methodology)

    William "Bubba" Head's approach examines whether SFSTs were administered according to NHTSA standards. Even small deviations from the standardized protocol — wrong instructions, uneven surface, inadequate demonstration — affect reliability. Body camera footage is critical here because it shows exactly how the tests were conducted.

    Traffic Stop Legality

    Every DWI case begins with a traffic stop. If the officer lacked reasonable suspicion for the stop, a motion to suppress can exclude everything that followed — including the BAC test. The police report and body camera footage document the officer's stated reason for the stop.

    "The number speaks for itself." The prosecution's lead argument will be the 0.09 BAC — presented as objective, scientific evidence that you were above the legal limit. They will frame the breathalyzer as reliable and the result as conclusive.

    How to think about this: An instrument registered 0.09. Whether that number accurately reflects your BAC at the time of driving depends on calibration, timing, and testing protocol. The number is the starting point of the conversation, not the end of it.

    "Officer observations confirm impairment." The prosecution will pair the BAC with the officer's observations — driving pattern, field sobriety test performance, demeanor, odor of alcohol — to build a totality argument.

    Appendix A: Brady/Giglio Checklist

    What This Is: Under Brady v. Maryland (1963) and Giglio v. United States (1972), the prosecution is constitutionally required to disclose evidence that is favorable to the defense.   • ☐ Evidence of witness bias or motive to lie
  • ☐ Lab reports, forensic analysis, chain of custody documentation
  • ☐ Surveillance footage, body camera footage, dashcam footage
  • ☐ 911 calls and dispatch records
  • ☐ Prior complaints against arresting officers

    Appendix C: Your Rights During Criminal Proceedings

    These rights exist regardless of your charge, your attorney, or your county.

    Constitutional Rights:

  • Right to remain silent (5th Amendment) — You cannot be compelled to testify against yourself
  • Right to an attorney (6th Amendment) — If you cannot afford one, one will be appointed
  • Right to a speedy trial (6th Amendment) — Timelines vary by state and jurisdiction
  • Right to confront witnesses (6th Amendment) — You can cross-examine anyone who testifies against you
  • Right against unreasonable search and seizure (4th Amendment) — Evidence obtained illegally may be suppressed
  • Right to a jury trial (6th Amendment) — For serious offenses, you have the right to be judged by a jury of your peers
  • Right to due process (14th Amendment) — Fair procedures must be followed
  • Right against double jeopardy (5th Amendment) — You cannot be tried twice for the same offense
  • Right to be presumed innocent — The prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt

    • Your Rights With Your Attorney:

  • You have the right to know what is happening in your case at all times
  • You have the right to be consulted before major decisions are made
  • You have the right to make the final decision on whether to accept a plea or go to trial
  • You have the right to effective assistance of counsel (Strickland v. Washington)
  • You have the right to change attorneys at any time (though timing and procedural requirements apply)

    • If You Feel Your Rights Are Being Violated:

  • Contact your state bar association's client protection hotline

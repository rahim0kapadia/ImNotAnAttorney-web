### 6i. Difficult Conversations

**Scenario 1: "The evidence is what it is, Roberto has injuries."**
- **What's happening:** Oversimplifying. He has scratches. You have bruising on your arms and neck.
- **What you can say:** "I understand he has injuries. But I also had injuries, bruising documented at booking. Has anyone compared the two? Scratches on his arm and bruises on my neck tell different stories about who was defending themselves."
- **Why it works:** Redirects to the evidence pattern rather than arguing about who's telling the truth.

**Scenario 2: "The plea offer is a good deal, deferred adjudication means no conviction."**
- **What's happening:** Technically true but critically misleading. Deferred adjudication for this charge type CANNOT be sealed in Texas.
- **What you can say:** "I understand deferred adjudication avoids a final conviction, but my research shows that DV deferred adjudication can't be sealed under Article 42A.111. That means it appears on background checks permanently. As a teacher, that distinction matters for my career. Can we explore whether the evidence supports a stronger position?"
- **Why it works:** Shows you understand the specific consequences. Reframes the conversation from "take the deal" to "is the deal actually good?"

**Scenario 3: "I haven't had time to review the footage."**
- **What's happening:** Caseload pressure. Common for public defenders.
- **What you can say:** "I understand your caseload is heavy. Can we set a specific date by which you'll have reviewed the recording and my booking medical records? The § 9.31 question is my biggest concern, and the answer depends on what the footage shows."
- **Why it works:** Creates accountability with empathy.

**Scenario 4: "The judge won't modify the order during a pending case."**
- **What's happening:** May be accurate for this specific judge, or may be a general assumption.
- **What you can say:** "Has Judge Gutierrez specifically declined to modify these orders in similar cases? My son and daughter are 4 and 7, and supervised contact is different from removing the order entirely. Can we at least make the request?"
- **Why it works:** Distinguishes between this judge's actual history and a generalized assumption.

Your April 28 hearing is a pre-trial hearing. In Bexar County, this is a case management proceeding, the judge, prosecution, and defense discuss case status, evidence exchange, pending motions, and whether the case is moving toward plea or trial. You may or may not be asked questions directly.

### Case Strategy

**Q1, The Deep-Dive Version of Your Golden Question:**
"Have you watched the full body camera footage from start to finish, not just the summary in the police report? Specifically, when the officers arrived, who appeared afraid, who was crying, who had visible injuries, and did the officers ask ME what happened before deciding to arrest me?"

**Why this is the question that matters most:** The footage captures what actually happened, not the officer's summary of what happened. Officers arrive, assess the scene, and write a report. The report may omit details that the footage preserves: your demeanor, your injuries, your husband's demeanor, the officers' decision-making process. If the footage shows officers arriving to find a frightened woman with bruises and a calm man with scratches, and then arresting the woman, that's your defense.

**A strong response sounds like:** "I've watched the footage. Here's what I see..." or "The footage shows [specific detail] that supports self-defense."

**If Whitfield says he hasn't watched it:** "This is the most important piece of evidence in my case. When can I expect you'll have reviewed it? The self-defense analysis can't happen without it."

**What to listen for:** Whether Whitfield is speaking from the footage or from the police report. An attorney who references specific moments in the video has done the work. One who talks only about what the report says is relying on the prosecution's version.

**Q2:** "What does Lenore Walker's research on defensive injuries say about cases like mine, and has anyone in the DA's office been trained on primary aggressor identification errors?"

**What you want to hear:** Recognition that wrongful arrest of DV victims is a known issue, and that the injury pattern in your case is consistent with established research.

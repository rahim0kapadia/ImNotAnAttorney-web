Understanding Your Charges

You told us you were charged with DWI in Harris County, Texas, after a checkpoint stop where you blew a .09 BAC.

Under Texas Penal Code § 49.04, DWI is defined as operating a motor vehicle in a public place while intoxicated. Here's what the prosecution must prove:

Element Prosecution Must ProvePlain EnglishQuestion for Your Attorney
Operation of a motor vehicleYou were driving or in physical control of the vehicle"Was I officially 'operating' the vehicle at the time of the stop, and does the checkpoint documentation support that?"
In a public placeThe location where you were driving is legally a public place"Does the checkpoint location qualify as a public place under the statute?"
While intoxicatedYour BAC was .08 or above, OR you lacked normal use of mental or physical faculties"Given my .09 reading and the breathalyzer's margin of error, how strong is the intoxication element?"
Penalty range (Tex. Penal Code § 49.04 — Class B misdemeanor, first offense): Up to 180 days in county jail, fine up to $2,000, license suspension of 90 days to 1 year (Tex. Transp. Code § 724.035). These are statutory maximums, not predictions. The questions in this report help you understand the realistic range for YOUR case. What this means: A first-offense DWI in Texas is a Class B misdemeanor. Most first offenders with no accident and no prior record face probation and community service rather than jail time — but the specifics depend on your county, your judge, and what your attorney negotiates. Your BAC of .09 is close enough to the .08 threshold that the breathalyzer's accuracy becomes a real question.

Your Rights in This Process

  • Right to see all evidence (discovery): Under Texas Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 39.14, you're entitled to review the prosecution's evidence, including breathalyzer calibration records, checkpoint protocols, and body camera footage.
  • Right to be consulted before any plea: Your attorney cannot accept or reject a plea offer without your knowledge and consent.
  • Right to understand your defense strategy: You're entitled to know what your attorney's plan is.
  • Right to request a different public defender: This is a procedural right, not an extreme step. If communication has broken down, you can request reassignment through the court.
  • Right to refuse a plea offer: No one can force you to plead guilty.

    Q2. Have you requested and reviewed the discovery in my case — specifically the breathalyzer calibration records, checkpoint authorization documents, and body camera footage?

    Why it matters: Justin McShane, the first attorney designated a "Forensic Lawyer Scientist" by the American Chemical Society, builds DWI defenses by attacking instrument precision. For a .09 reading, the calibration records of the specific breathalyzer device are critical — if the instrument was outside its calibration window or had a history of deviation, the .09 reading itself becomes unreliable. Under Texas Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 39.14, you're entitled to this discovery. Good answer: "Yes, I've requested discovery. Here's what we have so far and here's what's still outstanding..." If the answer is vague: "What specific discovery items have been requested, and what's the timeline for receiving them?" What to listen for: Whether the attorney distinguishes between having received discovery and having personally reviewed it. In high-volume practices, discovery may sit in a file unreviewed. The distinction matters.

    Q3. Walk me through the checkpoint — was it set up according to Texas checkpoint requirements, and is there anything we can challenge about the stop itself?

    Why it matters: William "Bubba" Head, voted Best DUI Attorney in America by NCDD, focuses on SFST administration errors and procedural gaps. DWI checkpoints in Texas have specific legal requirements for how they're established, operated, and supervised. If the checkpoint didn't follow proper protocol, the entire stop — and everything that followed — may be challengeable. Good answer: "I've reviewed the checkpoint authorization and here's what I found about how it was set up..." If the answer is vague: "Is there documentation showing the checkpoint was properly authorized, and have we obtained it?" What to listen for: Whether the attorney has actually reviewed the checkpoint authorization documents or is assuming they were done correctly.

    Q4. The officer said I failed the walk-and-turn. What does the body camera show, and are there grounds to challenge the field sobriety test administration?

    Why it matters: NHTSA's own studies show that the walk-and-turn test has a 32% false positive rate even when administered correctly (NHTSA DOT HS 809 839). If the officer didn't follow standardized administration procedures — instruction position, demonstration, counting method — the test results are even less reliable. Your body camera footage would show exactly what happened. Good answer: "I've watched the body camera footage and here's what I observed about how the tests were administered..." If the answer is vague: "Have you obtained and reviewed the body camera footage? If not, when can we do that together?" What to listen for: Whether the attorney can describe specific things from the footage, not just general impressions.

    Q5. What is the realistic outcome range for someone in my situation — first offense, no accident, .09 BAC, clean record — in Harris County specifically?

    Why it matters: Outcomes vary significantly by jurisdiction. Harris County has its own patterns for first-offense DWI cases. If someone describes the worst-case scenario — 180 days in jail — without explaining the typical outcome for your charge type and facts, ask: "What's the realistic range based on cases like mine in this jurisdiction?" That question tells you whether the penalty discussion is grounded in your specific facts or speaking in statutory maximums. Good answer: "In Harris County, a first-offense DWI with a .09, no accident, and no record typically resolves with [specific outcome range]. Here's what factors would push it higher or lower..." If the answer is vague: "I understand the statutory maximum. What I'm asking about is what actually happens in practice for cases like mine in this county." What to listen for: Specific reference to Harris County outcomes, not just Texas-wide statutory ranges.

    Q6. I'm a nurse — how does a DWI conviction affect my nursing license with the Texas Board of Nursing?

    Why it matters: Under the Texas Occupations Code and Texas Board of Nursing rules, a DWI conviction triggers reporting and potential disciplinary action. The Board evaluates each case individually, but the impact can range from probation to license suspension. Professional licensing consequences are often more devastating than the criminal penalty itself — and they're frequently not discussed until after a plea is entered. This is the consequence that matters most to you, and it should be part of any strategy discussion from the beginning. What to listen for: Whether the attorney addresses your nursing license as a central concern or treats it as a side issue. An attorney who understands professional licensing consequences will discuss them as part of the defense strategy, not as something to "deal with later."

    Q7. Are there any pretrial motions being considered — such as a motion to suppress the breathalyzer results or challenge the checkpoint stop?

    Why it matters: Suppression motions (motions asking the court to exclude evidence that was improperly obtained) challenge whether evidence was legally obtained. If the checkpoint stop or the breathalyzer administration had procedural problems, a motion to suppress could remove the key evidence from the case. These motions have filing deadlines, so timing matters. What to listen for: Whether the attorney has evaluated the case for motion opportunities or is following the standard resolution path. Not every case warrants a motion — but the evaluation should happen.

    Q8. Has anyone from the prosecution reached out about my case, and is there a standard offer on the table?

    Why it matters: Prosecutors have standard offers for most charge types. For a first-offense DWI in Harris County, there's typically a default starting offer. The question is whether the offer you eventually receive reflects your specific facts — a .09 with no accident and a clean record — or just the default for the charge category. If someone frames the standard offer as a personal victory they negotiated, it's worth knowing what every first offender receives. What to listen for: Whether the attorney can tell you what the standard offer is AND whether they've negotiated from that starting point.

    Q9. What's the status of my ALR hearing — was it requested within the 15-day window, and if not, what are the consequences?

If the deadline passed, your license may already be under administrative suspension — independent of what happens in the criminal case. What to listen for: A clear answer about whether the ALR hearing was requested and what the current status is.

    Q10. Could a rising BAC defense apply to my case — is it possible my BAC was below .08 at the time I was actually driving?

    Why it matters: Alcohol absorption takes time. If you had your last drink shortly before driving, your BAC may have been rising between the time you were driving and the time you were tested. The prosecution must prove you were intoxicated while driving, not at the time of the test. The strength of this argument depends on the exact timeline between your last drink, the stop, and the breath test — details your attorney can evaluate. What to listen for: Whether the attorney engages with the timeline specifics or dismisses the argument. With a .09 and "two glasses of wine over three hours," the timeline is worth evaluating.
